Chapter 12: Ethics & Societal Impact

Computing doesn't just change what's possible. It changes who has power, who gets left out, and how an industry actually runs.

In this chapter: 12.1 Five Ways Computing Reshapes Life 12.2 Reading Your Own Footprint Honestly 12.3 How AI Actually Works

12.1 Five Ways Computing Reshapes Life

A single piece of software rarely changes just one thing. A ride-share app doesn't just move people — it reshapes an entire local taxi economy, changes what "being available for work" means, and shifts who holds the data about a city's movement.

12.1.1 The Five Practices Computing Touches

Personal

How you relate to your own devices, time, and attention.

Ethical

What a tool makes easy to do — including harmful things.

Social

How people relate to each other differently because of it.

Economic

Who gets paid, who loses work, what gets automated.

Cultural

How shared norms and habits shift because of new tools.

Breaking It Down: Personal Impact

Personal impact is how a technology changes your own daily life: your time, your attention, your habits, and how you feel. It's the easiest impact to notice, because you experience it directly.

Some personal impacts are helpful. A calendar app reminds you about a test, and a map app means you never get lost. Others are harder to see. Many apps are designed to keep you scrolling as long as possible, because more time in the app means more ads shown. If you've ever opened an app "for one minute" and looked up an hour later, you've felt that design at work.

To judge personal impact honestly, ask two questions: "What does this tool make easier for me?" and "What is it quietly taking from me, like time, sleep, focus, or privacy?" Both answers are part of the real picture.

Breaking It Down: Ethical Impact

Ethics is about right and wrong: how we should treat each other. A technology's ethical impact is about what it makes easy to do, including harmful things. Tools don't make choices, but they can make some choices much easier than they used to be.

For example, a photo-editing app makes it easy to fix red eyes. It also makes it easy to edit a photo of someone to embarrass them. A group chat makes it easy to plan a party, and also easy to leave one person out on purpose where everyone can see. The tool is the same. What changes is how people use it.

When you look at a technology's ethics, ask: "What harmful action does this make faster, cheaper, or more anonymous than before?" Anything that makes harm easier deserves extra safeguards.

Breaking It Down: Social Impact

Social impact is about how technology changes the way people relate to each other: friends, families, neighbors, and strangers.

Technology can bring people together. A student who moves across the country can stay close to old friends through video calls, and people with rare health conditions can find support groups online. It can also push people apart. Families might sit in the same room, each on their own screen. Online arguments can get much harsher than face-to-face ones, because it's easier to be cruel to a username than to a person standing in front of you.

A good way to measure social impact is to ask: "Does this tool replace human connection, or does it add to it?" The same app can do either one, depending on how it's designed and how people use it.

Breaking It Down: Economic Impact

Economic impact is about money and work: who gets paid, who loses income, and which jobs change or disappear. Almost every major technology reshapes jobs.

Self-checkout machines are an easy example. They let stores hire fewer cashiers, which saves the store money. But it also means fewer entry-level jobs, the kind many teenagers get as a first job. At the same time, new jobs appear: people to repair the machines and people to write the software that runs them. Usually both things happen at once. Some jobs shrink, new ones grow, and the people losing jobs aren't always the ones who get the new ones.

When you judge economic impact, follow the money: "Who saves money? Who earns more? Who earns less? And who pays the hidden costs?"

Breaking It Down: Cultural Impact

Culture is a group's shared habits, values, and expectations, the "normal" way things are done. Technology changes culture slowly, and then suddenly everyone acts as if it was always that way.

Think about how people listen to music. Your grandparents may have bought records. Your parents may have bought CDs. Today most people stream almost any song instantly. That changed more than just the technology. It changed what people expect: we now expect every song, show, and answer to be available right away, for free or for a monthly fee.

Cultural impact is the hardest one to see while it's happening, because you're living inside it. A helpful trick is to ask an older relative: "How did people do this before?" The difference between their answer and your life is the cultural impact.

Worked Example: Tracing One App Through All Five

Personal: instant access to a ride, on your schedule.

Ethical: makes it easy to avoid paying into systems that fund traditional taxi regulation.

Social: fewer face-to-face interactions with a regular local driver.

Economic: drivers gain flexible income but lose the protections of traditional employment.

Cultural: "hailing a taxi" becomes an unfamiliar concept to an entire generation.

The trap: students often evaluate new technology on personal convenience alone — "it's faster, so it's good." A complete evaluation traces the effect across all five practices, including the ones that don't show up in your own daily experience of using the tool.

12.2 Reading Your Own Footprint Honestly

You covered digital identity back in Chapter 1. Revisit it now with a wider lens: not just "what does a stranger see about me," but "what does this reveal about how computing actually reshapes personal life at scale."

Breaking It Down: Your Footprint at Scale

Your digital footprint is the trail of information you leave behind online. Part of it is active: things you choose to share, like posts, comments, and photos. Part of it is passive: things collected about you without you actively sharing them, like your location history, the websites you visit, and how long you watch each video.

One piece of information on its own usually doesn't reveal much. But companies can combine many small pieces, which is called data aggregation. Your favorite stores, the time you wake up, the places you go every week, and the videos you like can be combined into a surprisingly detailed picture of who you are, what you can afford, and even what you might buy next.

That's what "at scale" means here. It's not just about one embarrassing post. It's about millions of people's small data points being collected, combined, bought, and sold. Understanding this helps you make smarter choices about your settings and what you share.

12.2.1 Reading a Real Privacy Policy

Every privacy policy is a small case study in economic impact — it names exactly what's collected, why, and who receives it, in language written by lawyers to be technically accurate and rarely alarming.

Breaking It Down: How to Read a Privacy Policy

A privacy policy is a document where a company explains what information it collects about you, why it collects it, and who it shares that information with. When you click "I agree" while signing up for an app, you're usually agreeing to its privacy policy, even if you never read it. Most people don't, because these policies are long and written in legal language.

You don't have to read every word. Look for three things. First, what is collected: your location, your contacts, your messages, what you click, how long you look at each post. Second, why: to make the app work, to show you ads, or to "improve services." Third, who receives it: watch for phrases like "third parties," "partners," or "affiliates." Those words mean other companies.

Why would a free app want so much information? Because for many free apps, information is the business. Advertisers pay more to show ads to people they know a lot about. There's a well-known saying in tech: "If you're not paying for the product, you might be the product."

Some of this collection is reasonable. A map app needs your location to give directions. The question to ask is whether what's collected matches what the app actually does. A flashlight app that wants your contacts and location is collecting far more than it needs.

Quick Check

A free flashlight app's privacy policy says it collects your location and contacts and shares them with "third-party partners." What two questions should you ask? (Answer: does the app actually need that data to work, and who are those partners? A flashlight needs neither, so it's collecting more than it needs.)

12.3 How AI Actually Works

You can't seriously evaluate AI's ethical and societal impact without knowing what's actually happening inside it. "It reproduces patterns in its training data" is a phrase you'll hear constantly about AI. It means something specific — here's what.

12.3.1 Traditional Programming vs. Machine Learning

A traditional program follows rules a human wrote explicitly — every "if this, then that" was typed by a programmer. A Machine Learning system flips that: instead of being told the rules, it's shown enormous amounts of training data and learns the underlying patterns itself. A spam filter isn't given a list of every possible scam phrase — it's shown thousands of real spam and real non-spam emails until it learns to tell the difference on its own.

Breaking It Down: Traditional Programming vs. Machine Learning

In traditional programming, a person writes every rule. Remember the grade calculator from Chapter 10: if the score is 90 or higher, then it's an A. The computer follows those rules exactly. It never does anything the programmer didn't write.

Machine learning (ML) works the opposite way. Instead of giving the computer the rules, you give it lots of examples along with the right answers, and it figures out the rules by itself. You show it 10,000 emails labeled "spam" or "not spam," and it slowly learns which patterns usually mean spam, like strange links, urgent warnings, or misspelled company names.

Why not just write the rules? Because for some problems, there are far too many rules to write. Scammers constantly change their wording, so a hand-written list of "spam phrases" would be out of date in a week. A machine learning system can keep learning from new examples.

There's a catch, and it connects straight back to Chapter 11: an ML system only learns from its examples. If the examples are incomplete, outdated, or unfair, the rules it learns will be too. That's why people say AI "reproduces patterns in its training data."

Neural Networks

A neural network is a machine learning structure loosely modeled after the brain's neurons — layers of small connected units, each adjusting how strongly it reacts to its inputs as it sees more training data. It's the technology behind most modern AI: recognizing images, understanding speech, generating text.

Breaking It Down: Neural Networks

A neural network is a kind of machine learning system made of many small connected units arranged in layers. It's "loosely" inspired by the brain, but don't take that too literally. It's really a big math machine, not a digital brain.

Here's a simple way to picture it. Information enters the input layer, for example the pixels of a photo. It passes through one or more hidden layers, where each unit looks for simple patterns, like an edge, a curve, or a patch of color. Later layers combine those simple patterns into bigger ones, like an eye, an ear, or whiskers. Finally, the output layer gives an answer: "cat, 92% sure."

How does it learn? Each connection between units has a strength, like a volume knob. During training, the network makes a guess, checks it against the right answer, and then turns all those knobs a tiny bit to be less wrong next time. Repeat that millions of times, and the network gets very good at the task.

The tradeoff is that with millions of knobs, even the people who built the network often can't explain exactly why it gave a particular answer. That's one reason AI is sometimes called a "black box," and why its decisions need careful checking.

12.3.2 What Neural Networks Actually Do

  • Computer Vision — lets a system interpret images, like a self-driving car recognizing a pedestrian stepping into the street.
  • Natural Language Processing (NLP) — lets a computer understand and generate human language, like a voice assistant understanding a spoken question and replying in plain language.
  • Recommendation Engines — predict what you'll want next based on patterns across millions of other users, like a music app or streaming service suggesting what to play next.
Breaking It Down: Computer Vision

Computer vision is the part of AI that lets computers "see," meaning they can recognize what's in a photo or video. To a computer, an image is just a giant grid of numbers, one number for each tiny dot of color (a pixel). Computer vision turns that grid of numbers into a meaning: "stop sign," "dog," "my friend's face."

You use it more than you might think. Your phone uses it to unlock with your face, sort your photo gallery, and add filters that stick to your face as you move. Doctors use it to help spot problems in X-rays. Self-driving cars use it to notice pedestrians, lanes, and traffic lights.

Computer vision is also where the face-recognition bias from Chapter 11 came from. When a vision system is trained mostly on certain kinds of faces, it sees those faces best. So the same question always applies: who was in the training data?

Breaking It Down: Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Natural language processing, or NLP, is the part of AI that works with human language, both written and spoken. "Natural" language means the language people actually use, full of slang, typos, and jokes, as opposed to the strict language of code.

NLP is behind voice assistants that understand spoken questions, autocorrect and predictive text on your keyboard, translation apps, and AI chatbots that write replies. Each one has to deal with how messy language is. "I'm dying" can mean "I'm laughing" or "I need help," depending on the situation.

Modern chatbots are trained on enormous amounts of text from the internet and books, and they learn to predict which words usually come next. That's why they can sound very confident and fluent, but also why they can make things up. Sounding right and being right are not the same thing.

Breaking It Down: Recommendation Engines

A recommendation engine predicts what you'll want next: the next video, song, product, or post. It's the system behind "Up Next," "For You," and "Customers also bought."

Most recommendation engines look for patterns across many users. Say thousands of people liked songs A and B, and most of them also liked song C. If you like A and B, the engine guesses you'll like C too. It also watches what you do. It notices what you replay, what you skip, and how long you pause on a post.

These engines are useful. But most are built to keep you watching or buying. They can also trap you in a filter bubble. That means you mostly see more of what you already like or believe. You rarely see anything new or different. Knowing how this works helps you notice when your feed is steering you.

Quick Check

What's the fundamental difference between traditional programming and machine learning? (Answer: traditional programming follows explicit rules a human wrote; machine learning learns its own patterns from training data.)

12.3.3 Game-Playing AI: State, Decision Trees & Minimax

A game-playing AI represents the exact current arrangement of the board at any moment as its state. To decide its next move, it can build a decision tree mapping out possible moves and the opponent's likely responses to each. Chess has too many possible future sequences to calculate all of them, so an AI can't just brute-force every option — for large games, it often uses a heuristic to estimate a good move without exhaustively checking every possibility.

Breaking It Down: State & Decision Trees

In a game, the state is a complete snapshot of everything that matters right now: where every piece is, whose turn it is, and the score. If you paused a game and took a photo of the board, that photo would capture the state.

A decision tree maps out what could happen next. Start with the current state at the top. Draw a branch for each move you could make. From each of those, draw branches for each move your opponent could make in response, and keep going. It looks like an upside-down tree, which is where the name comes from.

For a small game, a computer can draw the whole tree. Tic-tac-toe has only 255,168 possible games, which a computer can check in a blink. That's why a good tic-tac-toe AI never loses. Chess is another story. The number of possible chess games is so large that no computer could ever check them all.

So for big games, the AI only looks a few moves ahead. Then it uses a heuristic, a smart shortcut like those you learned about in Chapter 9, to estimate how good each position is. For example, it might count up the value of each player's pieces.

The Minimax algorithm is a specific strategy for this: it assumes the opponent will always make the smartest possible move, and picks the move that gives the best outcome even against that best-case opponent play.

Breaking It Down: How Minimax Thinks

Minimax is a strategy for two-player games. Its big idea is to assume your opponent is smart and will always make the move that's worst for you. So instead of hoping for the best, you plan for the worst and pick the move that still leaves you in the best position.

Here's how it works on a decision tree. For each move you could make, look at every response your opponent could make, and find the one that hurts you most. That's the score you should expect from that move. Then, out of all your moves, pick the one with the best of those worst-case scores. You're maximizing your result while your opponent tries to minimize it. That's where the name "mini-max" comes from.

You already think this way in real games. In chess or checkers, you don't make a move that looks great only if your opponent makes a silly mistake. You ask, "What's their best answer to this?" Minimax is that same careful thinking, done by a computer across many possible futures at once.

12.3.4 The Turing Test & Deepfakes

The Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing, evaluates whether a human judge, chatting blind with both a human and a machine, can reliably tell which is which — it's a test of convincing imitation, not of genuine understanding.

Breaking It Down: The Turing Test

Alan Turing was a British mathematician. During World War II, he helped break secret enemy codes. Many people call him one of the founders of computer science. In 1950, he asked a famous question: "Can machines think?" "Thinking" is hard to define, so he suggested a simple test instead. He called it the "imitation game." Today we call it the Turing Test.

The setup works like this. A human judge has typed conversations with two hidden partners: one is a person, and one is a machine. The judge doesn't know which is which. If the judge can't reliably tell them apart, the machine "passes."

Notice what the test really measures. It checks whether a machine can act like a human. It does not check whether the machine truly understands anything. Today's chatbots can be very convincing. That shows how good they are at imitation. It's also why imitation can be risky, as the next idea shows.

That same imitation capability raises a serious modern concern: deepfakes, AI-generated videos or audio realistic enough to convincingly show someone saying or doing something they never actually did — raising real ethical questions about consent, misinformation, and trust in recorded evidence itself.

Breaking It Down: Deepfakes

A deepfake is a fake video, image, or audio clip created with AI that makes it look or sound like a real person said or did something they never did. The name comes from "deep learning," a type of AI that uses large neural networks, plus "fake."

Deepfakes are made by training an AI on many real photos, videos, or voice recordings of a person until it can create new, realistic ones. A few years ago this took experts and powerful computers. Today some apps can copy a person's voice from a short recording. That's why scammers have used fake voices to call people pretending to be a family member in trouble and asking for money.

Deepfakes raise three big problems. Consent: people's faces and voices can be used without permission, sometimes to bully or humiliate them. Misinformation: a fake video of a leader saying something shocking can spread to millions of people before anyone checks it. Trust: when anything can be faked, people may start doubting real videos too.

Simple habits help. Be suspicious of shocking clips, especially ones that make you angry fast. Check whether trusted news sources are reporting the same thing. If a "family member" calls asking for money in an emergency, hang up and call them back on their real number. Some families even agree on a secret code word for real emergencies.
