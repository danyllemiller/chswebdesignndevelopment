Computing doesn't just change what's possible. It changes who has power, who gets left out, and how an industry actually runs.

12.1 Five Ways Computing Reshapes Life

A single piece of software rarely changes just one thing. A ride-share app doesn't just move people — it reshapes an entire local taxi economy, changes what "being available for work" means, and shifts who holds the data about a city's movement.

12.1.1 The Five Practices Computing Touches

Personal

How you relate to your own devices, time, and attention.

Ethical

What a tool makes easy to do — including harmful things.

Social

How people relate to each other differently because of it.

Economic

Who gets paid, who loses work, what gets automated.

Cultural

How shared norms and habits shift because of new tools.

Worked Example: Tracing One App Through All Five

Personal: instant access to a ride, on your schedule.

Ethical: makes it easy to avoid paying into systems that fund traditional taxi regulation.

Social: fewer face-to-face interactions with a regular local driver.

Economic: drivers gain flexible income but lose the protections of traditional employment.

Cultural: "hailing a taxi" becomes an unfamiliar concept to an entire generation.

The trap: students often evaluate new technology on personal convenience alone — "it's faster, so it's good." A complete evaluation traces the effect across all five practices, including the ones that don't show up in your own daily experience of using the tool.

12.3 How AI Actually Works

You can't seriously evaluate AI's ethical and societal impact without knowing what's actually happening inside it. "It reproduces patterns in its training data" (12.2's Quick Check) means something specific — here's what.

12.3.1 Traditional Programming vs. Machine Learning

A traditional program follows rules a human wrote explicitly — every "if this, then that" was typed by a programmer. A Machine Learning system flips that: instead of being told the rules, it's shown enormous amounts of training data and learns the underlying patterns itself. A spam filter isn't given a list of every possible scam phrase — it's shown thousands of real spam and real non-spam emails until it learns to tell the difference on its own.

Neural Networks

A neural network is a machine learning structure loosely modeled after the brain's neurons — layers of small connected units, each adjusting how strongly it reacts to its inputs as it sees more training data. It's the technology behind most modern AI: recognizing images, understanding speech, generating text.

12.3.2 What Neural Networks Actually Do

  • Computer Vision — lets a system interpret images, like a self-driving car recognizing a pedestrian stepping into the street.
  • Natural Language Processing (NLP) — lets a computer understand and generate human language, like a voice assistant understanding a spoken question and replying in plain language.
  • Recommendation Engines — predict what you'll want next based on patterns across millions of other users, like a music app or streaming service suggesting what to play next.
Quick Check

What's the fundamental difference between traditional programming and machine learning? (Answer: traditional programming follows explicit rules a human wrote; machine learning learns its own patterns from training data.)

12.3.3 Game-Playing AI: State, Decision Trees & Minimax

A game-playing AI represents the exact current arrangement of the board at any moment as its state. To decide its next move, it can build a decision tree mapping out possible moves and the opponent's likely responses to each. Chess has too many possible future sequences to calculate all of them, so an AI can't just brute-force every option — for large games, it often uses a heuristic to estimate a good move without exhaustively checking every possibility.

The Minimax algorithm is a specific strategy for this: it assumes the opponent will always make the smartest possible move, and picks the move that gives the best outcome even against that best-case opponent play.

12.3.4 The Turing Test & Deepfakes

The Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing, evaluates whether a human judge, chatting blind with both a human and a machine, can reliably tell which is which — it's a test of convincing imitation, not of genuine understanding.

That same imitation capability raises a serious modern concern: deepfakes, AI-generated videos or audio realistic enough to convincingly show someone saying or doing something they never actually did — raising real ethical questions about consent, misinformation, and trust in recorded evidence itself.
