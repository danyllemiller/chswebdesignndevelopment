Chapter 12: Ethics & Societal Impact

Computing doesn't just change what's possible. It changes who has power, who gets left out, and how an industry actually runs.

In this chapter: 12.1 Five Ways Computing Reshapes Life 12.2 Reading Your Own Footprint Honestly

12.1 Five Ways Computing Reshapes Life

A single piece of software rarely changes just one thing. A ride-share app doesn't just move people — it reshapes an entire local taxi economy, changes what "being available for work" means, and shifts who holds the data about a city's movement.

12.1.1 The Five Practices Computing Touches

Personal

How you relate to your own devices, time, and attention.

Ethical

What a tool makes easy to do — including harmful things.

Social

How people relate to each other differently because of it.

Economic

Who gets paid, who loses work, what gets automated.

Cultural

How shared norms and habits shift because of new tools.

Worked Example: Tracing One App Through All Five

Personal: instant access to a ride, on your schedule.

Ethical: makes it easy to avoid paying into systems that fund traditional taxi regulation.

Social: fewer face-to-face interactions with a regular local driver.

Economic: drivers gain flexible income but lose the protections of traditional employment.

Cultural: "hailing a taxi" becomes an unfamiliar concept to an entire generation.

The trap: students often evaluate new technology on personal convenience alone — "it's faster, so it's good." A complete evaluation traces the effect across all five practices, including the ones that don't show up in your own daily experience of using the tool.

Activity: The Cross-Discipline Brief

Pick one algorithm you can actually name and explain — a recommendation algorithm (like the one behind a "For You" feed), a matching algorithm (like ride-share driver matching), or a scheduling algorithm (like the one behind class-period assignments). If you're stuck, pick one of those three.

  1. Find and describe three genuinely different fields or industries where a version of your algorithm is actually used (medicine, entertainment, hiring, transportation, agriculture — pick three real, different ones).
  2. For each of the three, write 2–3 sentences on specifically how it's applied there.
  3. Using the Five Practices framework above, write a one-page brief on your algorithm's potential impact.
  4. Your brief must name one real, specific benefit and one real, specific harm — not a vague "it could be good or bad."

Turn In: Your algorithm, your three field applications, and your one-page impact brief with a named benefit and harm.

12.2 Reading Your Own Footprint Honestly

You covered digital identity back in Chapter 1. Revisit it now with a wider lens: not just "what does a stranger see about me," but "what does this reveal about how computing actually reshapes personal life at scale."

12.2.1 Reading a Real Privacy Policy

Every privacy policy is a small case study in economic impact — it names exactly what's collected, why, and who receives it, in language written by lawyers to be technically accurate and rarely alarming.

Quick Check

Name one honest limit of an AI system worth remembering before you evaluate its societal impact. (Answer: it reproduces patterns in its training data and can't explain itself by default.)

Activity: The Privacy Audit

Revisit your Chapter 1 Footprint Audit, but this time through the economic-impact lens from this section.

  1. Pick one app or service you actually use, search for its real privacy policy, and list at least 5 specific things it collects, why it says it collects them, and who it shares them with.
  2. Search your own name the way you did in Chapter 1, and list what a stranger can currently find about you.
  3. Fix at least one thing you find — a setting that should be private, an old post you don't want representing you.

Turn In: The privacy policy you read (what it collects, why, who receives it), what you found searching your own name, and what you fixed.
