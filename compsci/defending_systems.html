Why does the most secure possible system usually fail the moment it meets a real user?

19.1 The Usability-Security Tradeoff

A system requiring a 40-character password, changed every week, is extremely secure on paper. In practice, users write it on a sticky note stuck to the monitor. Security nobody will actually use protects nothing.

19.1.1 The Measures, and What They Cost

Unique Passwords + Manager
Multi-Factor Authentication
Encryption at Rest & in Transit
Least-Privilege Access

Plus: firewalls, regular patching, and backups.

Worked Example: Recommending by Scenario

A school, a hospital, and a game server have completely different threat models and completely different acceptable friction. The hospital tolerates far more login friction than the game server would, because the cost of a breach is far higher. The "right" security measure isn't fixed — it's scored against the specific scenario every time.

The trap: students often think stronger security is always better. Every control adds friction, and users defeat controls that are too painful — forced frequent password changes is the classic example, since it reliably drives people to write passwords down instead of memorizing something stronger.

19.1.2 Why Backups Are a Security Control

Backups don't stop an attack. They're the only reliable recovery path after one — particularly ransomware, where your only real alternative to a clean backup is paying the attacker.

Quick Check

Name one security measure users commonly defeat, and why. (Answer: forced frequent password changes — they drive people to write passwords down, which is worse than the original risk.)

19.2 Writing a Recommendation Someone Will Fund

A security recommendation written in dense jargon and one written for a business owner can carry the exact same technical facts. Only one of them actually gets funded.

19.2.1 The Structure That Gets Read

Every real recommendation needs five parts, in plain language: the risk, its likelihood and impact, the recommended control, what it costs in money and friction, and what happens if it's declined.

Worked Example: Rewriting Jargon Into Business Language

Jargon version: "Implement MFA across all privileged accounts to mitigate credential-stuffing exposure."

Business version: "Right now, one leaked password from another site could let someone into our admin accounts. Adding a second login step blocks that — it costs a small amount of extra login time, and skipping it leaves that door open."

The trap: students often think declining a security control is always the wrong call. It isn't — declining is a legitimate business risk decision. Your job as the one making the recommendation is to make that decision an informed one, not to make it for them.

Quick Check

What five things belong in a real security recommendation? (Answer: the risk in plain language, its likelihood and impact, the recommended control, its cost, and the consequence of declining it.)

The Small Business: Sierra Nevada Pet Supply

A local pet store with 8 employees, one shared computer at the checkout register, and one back-office computer the owner uses for orders and payroll. They store customer names, emails, and order history (customer credit card numbers go through a separate outside payment processor, not their own system).

Their current setup: the store's Wi-Fi password hasn't changed in 3 years and is written on a sticky note behind the register. Every employee logs into the same shared account on the checkout computer — nobody has their own login. There is no backup system for the order and customer records. Software updates get installed "whenever someone remembers."
