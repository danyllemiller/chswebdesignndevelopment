Spreadsheet Basics, From Zero

If you've never really used a spreadsheet before, start here — everything else in this chapter assumes you already know what's on this page.

Rows, Columns, and Cells

A spreadsheet is a grid. Columns run up and down and are labeled with letters (A, B, C...). Rows run left to right and are labeled with numbers (1, 2, 3...). Where one column and one row meet is a single box called a cell — everything you type in a spreadsheet goes into a cell.

Cell References: A1 Notation

Every cell has an address made of its column letter, then its row number — no space between them. The cell in column A, row 1 is called A1. The cell in column C, row 4 is called C4. Click any cell in a real spreadsheet and its address shows up in the box at the top-left corner, called the Name Box — that's how you can always check you're looking at the cell you think you are.

Typing Data vs. Writing a Formula

Click a cell and start typing, and whatever you type just sits there as text or a number — that's data. Press Enter to move down to the next cell, or Tab to move right.

A formula is different: it always starts with an equals sign (=), and instead of showing what you typed, the cell shows the result of a calculation.

Worked Example: Your First Formula

Cell A1 contains 10. Cell B1 contains 5.

Click cell C1 and type =A1+B1, then press Enter. C1 doesn't show the text "A1+B1" — it shows 15, the actual sum of whatever is really inside A1 and B1 right now. Change A1 to 20 and C1 instantly recalculates to 25, with nothing retyped. That's the entire point of a spreadsheet over a calculator: the formula keeps working even after the numbers change.

The four basic math operators work exactly like you'd expect: + add, - subtract, * multiply, / divide. =C4*2 doubles whatever is in C4. =(A1+A2+A3)/3 averages three cells by hand, the long way (Chapter 7.1 covers the built-in function that does this in one word instead).

Sheets and Tabs

One spreadsheet file can hold multiple grids at once, called sheets or tabs — you'll see them as labeled tabs along the bottom edge of the screen, exactly like tabs in a web browser. Click + next to the existing tabs to add a new one, and double-click a tab's name to rename it. This matters directly for your Unit 3 Project: it asks for a workbook with four specifically-named tabs inside one file, not four separate files.

Quick Check

Cell B2 contains the number 8. You type B2*3 (no equals sign) into cell C2. What shows up in C2? (Answer: the literal text "B2*3" -- without the leading =, Sheets treats it as plain text, not a formula, so it never calculates anything.)

Practice before you need it: open a blank Google Sheet (sheet.new), type a few numbers into column A, and write one formula in column B that adds, subtracts, multiplies, or divides two of them. Rename Sheet1 to something else just to see how. None of this is turned in — it's just getting your hands on the tool before 7.1 starts building on top of it.

Why This Matters: Big Data

Fifteen rows of quiz scores is a spreadsheet. A retail chain's fifteen million daily transactions, a hospital network's patient records, or a social media platform's billions of posts a day is something else entirely — that's Big Data: extremely large data sets that must be analyzed computationally to reveal patterns, trends, and associations, especially relating to human behavior.

You can't scroll through fifteen million rows looking for a trend by eye. Every skill in this chapter — a formula that runs once and applies to every row, a chart that answers one clear question, a dashboard that lets someone filter down to just what they need — exists because of Big Data. They're the same tools whether the sheet has 15 rows or 15 million; you're just about to find out why they matter at all.

Volume

The sheer amount of data — often too much for a human to read, only to compute

Velocity

How fast new data arrives — a sensor or app can generate new rows every second

Variety

Numbers, text, images, location, timestamps — rarely just one tidy column type

A Real Example

A streaming service doesn't watch you personally decide what to recommend next. It analyzes patterns across millions of viewers' watch histories — computationally, because no team of humans could read that much data — to find "people who watched X also watched Y." That's Big Data analysis producing one small, useful recommendation.

7.1 Functions & References

A spreadsheet formula is a program. Write the rule once, and it applies to every row without you touching it again — that's the entire point.

7.1.1 Relative vs. Absolute References

Copy a formula down a column and its cell references usually shift to match — that's a relative reference. Sometimes you need one reference to stay locked in place no matter where you copy the formula — that's an absolute reference, marked with a dollar sign like $B$2.

The Trap: Breaking the Reference Type

You build a formula that multiplies each row's sales by a tax rate in cell B2. Copy it down without locking B2 as absolute, and every row silently references a different, empty cell instead of your tax rate — producing confident, wrong answers across the entire sheet. It won't error. It'll just be quietly wrong.

7.1.2 The Function Families

Aggregate

SUM, AVERAGE, COUNT

Logical

IF, AND, OR

Lookup

VLOOKUP, INDEX/MATCH

Text

CONCATENATE, TRIM

Always verify a new formula against a small sample you calculated by hand before trusting it on 2,000 rows. It costs thirty seconds and it's the only thing standing between you and a wrong number nobody catches.

7.2 Data Visualisation

A chart can be built from completely accurate numbers and still lie to you. The distortion doesn't come from fake data — it comes from design choices.

7.2.1 Choosing the Right Chart

Every chart answers one of five question types. Pick the wrong one and the chart looks fine but answers a question nobody asked:

  • Comparison — bar charts
  • Composition — pie or stacked bar charts
  • Distribution — histograms
  • Relationship — scatter plots
  • Change over time — line charts
Worked Example: The Truncated Axis

A bar chart with a Y-axis that starts at 90 instead of 0 can make a 2-point difference look like a massive gap. Same numbers, same bars, completely different story — just by moving where the axis starts. That's the whole trick behind most misleading charts you'll ever see.

The trap: students often think a chart is only "wrong" if it uses fake numbers. It doesn't need to. A truncated axis, an inconsistent scale, or a 3D effect that distorts area can all mislead a reader while every single number on the chart stays technically correct.

7.2.2 Interactive Dashboards

A static chart answers your question. An interactive one — with filters, slicers, and linked charts — lets the reader ask their own. Good dashboard layout puts the most important number first, groups related charts together, and never shows more than someone can read at a glance.

Quick Check

7.3 From Raw Data to a Clear Story

A spreadsheet full of numbers isn't automatically useful. It has to be found, cleaned up, dug through for a real pattern, and finally explained to someone else — and each of those steps has its own name and its own way of going wrong.

7.3.1 Data Mining & Data Cleaning

Data mining is the process of digging through a large dataset — software scanning years of sales records — to uncover a pattern no human noticed just by looking. It only works, though, if the data going in is trustworthy, which is why data cleaning (or "scrubbing") almost always comes first: removing duplicate entries, fixing missing or inconsistent fields, and throwing out anything obviously broken, before you trust a single number that comes out the other end.

The Trap: Bias Before You Even Start

A survey about phone habits that's only sent to people over 60 will produce numbers — but they won't represent everyone. This is bias: a flaw in how data was collected that skews the results before any analysis even begins. No formula or chart can fix bad data collection after the fact.

7.3.2 Outliers & Correlation

Once your data is clean, two ideas come up constantly when looking for patterns:

  • Outlier — a data point far outside the normal range. In a set of test scores mostly between 70–95, one score of 12 is an outlier. It might be a genuine result worth investigating, or it might be a data-entry error — either way, it's worth a second look before you include it in an average.
  • Correlation — when two things rise and fall together, without one necessarily causing the other. Ice cream sales and drowning incidents both rise in the summer; ice cream doesn't cause drowning — both are driven by a third factor (hot weather). "Correlation isn't causation" is the single most important sentence in data analysis.
Quick Check

Two values in a dataset rise and fall together, but one doesn't cause the other. What's this relationship called? (Answer: a correlation.)

7.3.3 Pivot Tables & Communicating a Claim

A pivot table lets you instantly reorganize and summarize thousands of rows — totaled by region, by month, by product — without touching or duplicating the original data. It's the fastest way to go from a wall of raw rows to a specific answer.

All of this — mining, cleaning, spotting outliers and correlations, pivoting — is in service of one final step: using data to communicate a clear claim. That means picking the specific numbers and chart that support the point you're making, and presenting them so someone else reaches the same conclusion you did, on their own, at a glance.
