What your data is worth, who's collecting it, and the difference between using someone's work and stealing it.

2.1 Personal Data & Privacy

Every privacy policy uses the same handful of words — encryption, cookies, HTTPS — and most people click "Accept" without knowing what any of them actually do. That's the whole business model. You can't defend data you don't understand.

2.1.1 The Protective Vocabulary

Encryption

Scrambling data with a key so that anyone who intercepts it sees nonsense instead of your actual information. Only someone with the right key can unscramble it.

HTTPS

The "S" means the connection between you and the site is encrypted. A padlock icon in the address bar is your signal that the trip your data takes is private.

Cookies

Small files a website leaves in your browser to remember you — your login, your cart, or which ads it thinks you'll click.

Password Strength

Length matters more than symbols. correcthorsebatterystaple takes longer to crack than P@ss1! because it's simply longer.

Breaking It Down: Encryption & HTTPS

Encryption is like writing a note in a secret code before passing it across the classroom. Anyone who grabs the note in the middle sees only nonsense. Only your friend, who has the key to the code, can read it. Computers do this with math, scrambling data so thoroughly that it's practically impossible to unscramble without the right key.

HTTPS is encryption for websites. When a web address starts with https:// and shows a padlock icon, the information traveling between you and that site, like your password or credit card number, is encrypted on the way. The plain http:// version, without the S, sends data in a form others could read.

One important limit: the padlock means the connection is private, not that the website is honest. A scam site can have a padlock too. So HTTPS protects your data on the trip, but you still need to check that you're on the real website before typing anything in.

Breaking It Down: Cookies

A cookie is a small file a website saves in your browser so it can remember you. Some cookies are helpful. They keep you logged in, remember what's in your shopping cart, and save your language or dark-mode setting.

Other cookies are used for tracking. Third-party cookies come from companies other than the site you're visiting, usually advertisers. Because the same ad company appears on many different websites, its cookies can follow you from site to site. That's how you can look at sneakers on one website and then see sneaker ads on a completely different one.

That's what those "Accept cookies?" pop-ups are about. Privacy laws require many sites to ask. You can often choose "Reject all" or "Necessary only" to keep the helpful cookies and block the tracking ones. You can also clear your cookies in your browser settings, though that will log you out of sites.

2.1.2 What Gets Collected, and How

Cookies are the visible part. Behind them are trackers embedded in ads and share buttons across thousands of sites, and device fingerprinting — identifying your specific device by its unique combination of screen size, fonts, and browser settings, without needing a cookie at all.

Worked Example: One Click, Traced

You click "Accept" on a cookie banner. Here's what actually happens next:

  1. The site drops a cookie in your browser tied to a unique ID.
  2. Every ad and tracking pixel embedded on that page reads that ID too.
  3. Those trackers follow the same ID across other sites that use the same ad network.
  4. A profile builds: what you looked at, how long, what you clicked next — sold to advertisers who never asked you directly.

The trap: students often think the data they typed is the only data collected. In practice, inferred data — conclusions drawn from your behavior rather than anything you typed — is often more revealing. Nobody told a site your income bracket, but your browsing pattern can guess it close enough to sell.

2.1.3 The Limits of Data Management

Be honest with yourself about what privacy settings can and can't undo:

  • Deletion is often partial. "Delete my account" frequently means "hide it," not "erase it from every backup."
  • Breaches are permanent. Once your password or email appears in a leaked database, it's out there forever — changing your password doesn't un-leak the old one.
  • No setting removes data already sold. Turning off ad tracking today doesn't recall the profile a broker already built and sold last year.
Quick Check

A site collects your browsing history and uses it to guess your age range without you ever entering your birthday. Is that data volunteered, observed, or inferred? (Answer: inferred — nobody typed it in, it was concluded from behavior.)

Activity: The Data Trail

Pick one app or website you actually use (a game, a social app, a streaming service).

  1. Search "[the app's name] privacy policy" and open the real one. Skim it — you don't need to read every word, but find the section on data collection.
  2. List at least 5 specific things it says it collects, in your own words, and why it says it collects each one.
  3. List who it says the data gets shared with (advertisers, "partners," other companies).
  4. On that same site, open your browser's cookie/site-data settings (search "how to view cookies in [your browser]" if you're not sure how) and count how many different third-party domains are tracking you on that one page.

Turn In: The app you chose, your list of what it collects and why, who it shares with, and your third-party cookie count.

2.2 Intellectual Property & Attribution

You found the perfect image for a project. Can you use it? The honest answer is: it depends on a license you probably haven't read yet.

2.2.1 Copyright, Permission & Fair Use

The moment someone creates an original work — a photo, a song, a paragraph — copyright protects it automatically. No registration required. That means the default answer to "can I use this?" is no, unless one of three things is true: you have permission, the work is licensed for reuse, or your use qualifies as fair use.

The Trap: "Fair Use" Is Not a Permission Slip

Students often think fair use is something you can decide for yourself in advance, like a coupon. It isn't. Fair use is a legal defense you raise after you've already been sued for using something — weighed after the fact on factors like purpose, amount used, and market impact. It's a possible argument in court, not a green light beforehand.

Breaking It Down: How Fair Use Is Decided

Fair use is a part of U.S. copyright law that allows limited use of copyrighted work without permission in some situations, like criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, and research. But there's no simple rule like "10 seconds is fine." Courts weigh four factors every time.

1. Purpose: are you commenting on, teaching about, or transforming the work into something new, or just copying it to entertain or make money? 2. Nature of the work: using facts or published news is more likely to be fair than using a creative work like a song or movie. 3. Amount: did you use a small piece or the most important part, sometimes called the "heart" of the work? 4. Market effect: could your use replace the original and cost the creator money?

For example, quoting two lines of a poem in an essay that analyzes it leans toward fair use. Posting the entire album on your own channel does not, even with "no copyright intended" in the description. That phrase has no legal power. When you're unsure, the safest choices are to get permission, use work with a license that allows reuse, or create your own.

2.2.2 Correct Attribution

When a work's license does allow reuse, you still owe the creator credit. A correct attribution has four parts:

Title

What the work is called

Author

Who made it

Source

Where you found it

License

What you're allowed to do

Worked Example: Right vs. Wrong Attribution

Wrong: "Photo from the internet"

Wrong: "Credit: unknown"

Right: "'Desert Sunset' by J. Alvarez, via Unsplash, CC0 license"

You'll also see Creative Commons codes on licensed work — each one adds an obligation. BY means you must credit the creator. SA ("share-alike") means anything you build from it must carry the same license forward. NC means non-commercial use only. Read the code before you reuse the work, not after.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Creative Commons Is a License

Creative Commons is a set of free licenses that creators attach to their work to say exactly how others may use it (like BY, SA, and NC). It is not a website, a forum, or a group of artists.

There's a real argument on both sides of IP law worth knowing honestly: it funds creators and protects the investment behind their work — but it can also lock up culture and block people from building on ideas that came before them. Both things are true at once.

Activity: The Attribution Lab

Find three real, reusable assets — photos, icons, or music — from free sites like Unsplash, Pexels, or a Creative-Commons search.

  1. For each of the three, check the page and confirm you're actually licensed to use it (not just any image you found by searching).
  2. Write a correct four-part attribution for each one, matching the "Right" format above: Title, Author, Source, License.
  3. Now find two examples of bad attribution — either the "Wrong" examples above, or real ones you find online — and rewrite each as a correct four-part attribution using whatever real information you can track down about them.

Turn In: Your three correct attributions (with links to the original assets) and your two fixed bad attributions.

2.3 The Data You Never Chose to Give

Privacy conversations usually focus on what you post. The bigger risk is what's generated about you automatically — data you never typed, never saw a form for, and never got asked about.

2.3.1 Volunteered, Observed & Inferred

Not all data is the same kind of exposed:

  • Volunteered: you typed it — your name in a signup form.
  • Observed: collected directly from your action — your location while an app is open.
  • Inferred: concluded from patterns — a guess about your interests, habits, or even mood based on what you clicked and when.

Inference is where the real exposure sits, because it's invisible. You can review what you typed. You can't review a conclusion a system drew about you behind the scenes.

Breaking It Down: The Three Kinds of Data, Up Close

Volunteered data is information you knowingly give: your name, email, and birthday on a sign-up form, or a post you write. It's the most visible kind, because you typed it, so you know it exists.

Observed data is collected by watching what you do, often automatically: your location while an app is open, which videos you watch and for how long, what you search for, what time you're usually online. You didn't fill anything out, but your actions created a record.

Inferred data is a guess a company makes by combining the other two. Say you watch lots of workout videos, search for running shoes, and go online at 6 a.m. A system might label you "fitness fan, early riser" and show you matching ads. These guesses can be about very personal things, like your mood or money. They can also be wrong. And you usually can't see them at all. That's why this chapter calls them the biggest hidden risk.

2.3.2 Weighing the Tradeoff

Automated data collection isn't automatically bad — it's a tradeoff, and the same tradeoff resolves differently depending on which value you weight most:

Worked Example: Location Tracking

Argued for safety: if something happens to you, your location history could help someone find you fast.

Argued for law: that same history can be subpoenaed and used against you in ways you never agreed to.

Argued for ethics: should a company be allowed to sell where you sleep and work, even if it's technically legal?

The trap: students often think there's one correct answer to a privacy tradeoff. There usually isn't — there's a decision about which value you're willing to give up ground on, made honestly instead of by default.

Quick Check

Name one system that generates data about you without you ever deliberately entering anything. (Example answer: a fitness app inferring your sleep schedule from when your phone stops moving.)

Activity: The Privacy Position

Pick one real privacy tradeoff — location tracking (like the Worked Example above), school security cameras, an app that listens for your voice commands, or one of your own.

  1. Write one argument FOR it from the safety angle — who does it protect, and how?
  2. Write one argument AGAINST it from the legal angle — how could this data be used against someone in a way they didn't expect?
  3. Write one argument that questions it from the ethical angle — should this be allowed even if it's technically legal?
  4. State your own actual position in 2–3 sentences — not "it depends," an actual stance.
  5. Write the single strongest objection someone could raise against your position, and respond to it honestly.

Turn In: Your tradeoff, your three angles, your position, and your response to the strongest objection.

2.4 PII, Algorithms & Public Consequences

Three more pieces close the loop between the data collected about you (2.1/2.3), what you post (Chapter 1), and the real-world consequences both can carry.

2.4.1 Personally Identifiable Information (PII)

Personally Identifiable Information (PII) is any data that could be used, alone or combined with other data, to identify a specific individual — a full name plus a home address, a Social Security number, or a photo tagged with a location. A single piece might seem harmless (plenty of people share a first name), but PII becomes dangerous exactly when pieces get combined into something that points to one specific real person.

Breaking It Down: Protecting Your PII

Some PII is sensitive enough to be dangerous all by itself. Think of your Social Security number, passwords, bank card numbers, and health records. Other PII seems harmless alone, like your school, your birthday, your town, or your pet's name. But put those small pieces together, and someone might find you, guess your security questions, or pretend to be you.

Think about what a stranger could learn from one public profile. They see your full name. They see your school's mascot in your photos. They see a birthday post and a photo tagged at your favorite park. Put together, that tells them where you are most afternoons. Identity thieves and online predators look for exactly this.

Some habits help. Keep your accounts private. Turn off location tags on photos. Skip online quizzes that ask for your first pet or your street, since those are common security questions. Share private details only on trusted sites with HTTPS. Before you post, ask: "What could someone figure out by adding this to what I've already shared?"

2.4.2 Algorithms, Personalization & Echo Chambers

The same tracking data from 2.1 doesn't just get sold — platforms use it to personalize what you see. A shopping site remembering what you looked at yesterday and showing similar ads today is the same underlying mechanism as a social feed showing you more of what you already engage with. The downside is an echo chamber: an algorithm optimized to keep you engaged tends to show you more of what you already agree with, narrowing what you see over time and making opposing views feel rarer than they actually are.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Trackers Cut Both Ways

Data-collection tools personalize what you see, but they can also build echo chambers and weaken your privacy. They don't guarantee anyone equal access to anything.

Quick Check

What's the downside of an algorithm that keeps showing you content similar to what you already engage with? (Answer: it can create an echo chamber, narrowing what you see and making differing views feel rarer than they really are.)

2.4.3 Public Posts Carry Different Legal Weight

This chapter's privacy protections (2.1) work in the other direction too: a public social media post can create real legal consequences — for defamation, harassment, or copyright — in a way a private, one-on-one conversation usually doesn't, because a public post is published, potentially reaching (and provably influencing or harming) a wide audience, while a private conversation stays between the people in it. It's also exactly the same "sum of everything visible" idea from Chapter 1's digital footprint — a college admissions officer searching your name is looking at your public record, not anything you said privately, and that public record can affect real future opportunities.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Public Posts vs. Private Talk

A public post is published: it spreads, it lasts, and it can bring real legal consequences for defamation or threats. A private conversation usually stays between the people in it. Nobody signs a contract for each post; the difference is that public posts are public and permanent.

Activity: The Algorithm Audit

Look at your own real feed on a platform you use.

  1. List five pieces of information that, combined, would count as PII for you specifically — then note which of those you've actually posted publicly somewhere.
  2. Scroll your own social feed for two minutes and write down a pattern you notice — is it showing you a narrow range of opinions/topics, or a genuinely wide one? Give three specific examples from what you saw.
  3. Name one real account or search you could deliberately follow to break out of your own likely echo chamber, and explain why you picked it.
  4. Describe one real difference between something you'd post publicly versus say in a private group chat, and explain the legal or reputational reasoning behind that choice.

Turn In: Your PII list with what's already public, your feed-pattern observations, your echo-chamber-breaking suggestion, and your public-vs-private example.
