The same algorithm shows up in medicine, agriculture, and music. That's not a coincidence — it's the whole reason algorithmic thinking is worth learning once.

13.1 One Algorithm, Many Fields

A sorting algorithm doesn't know or care whether it's sorting hospital patients by urgency, crop yields by field, or songs by tempo. The logic transfers completely — only the data and the stakes change.

13.1.1 The Transferability Insight

Medicine

A classification algorithm flags which scans need urgent human review first.

Agriculture

The same kind of classification flags which crop rows show early signs of disease.

Music

The same underlying logic groups songs into a playlist by matching similar patterns.

Worked Example: Mapping One Algorithm Onto Three Disciplines

Take a matching algorithm — the kind that pairs two things based on shared attributes. In healthcare, it matches patients to donors. In agriculture, it matches seed varieties to soil conditions. In music, it matches listeners to recommended songs. What changes: the data and the consequences of a wrong match. What stays identical: the algorithm's core logic.

The trap: students often think each field needs its own specialized programming knowledge from scratch. Often it doesn't — the same handful of algorithmic patterns (sorting, matching, classification) reappear constantly across completely unrelated industries, which is exactly why "learn to code" transfers so widely.

13.2 AI's Impact on Society, Honestly

Talking about AI tends to collapse into one of two extremes: it will fix everything, or it will replace everyone. Neither is an honest answer. The real impact is more specific — and more useful to actually understand.

13.2.1 The Honest Limits

What AI Actually Is and Isn't
  • It reproduces patterns present in its training data — it doesn't reason from scratch.
  • It can't explain itself by default — a confident answer isn't the same as a justified one.
  • It's accountable to whoever deployed it, not to you — a wrong answer is still someone's responsibility.

13.2.2 Impacts Beyond Job Displacement

"AI will take jobs" is the headline everyone's heard. It's real, but it's also not the whole picture — and it crowds out impacts that matter just as much:

  • Accountability gaps — when an automated decision harms someone, who's actually responsible?
  • Unequal access — the most powerful tools often cost the most, widening gaps that already existed.
  • Concentration of power — the data and computing resources needed to build advanced systems sit with a small number of organizations.
