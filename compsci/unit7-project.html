Unit 7: Networks and the Internet — Project

The Network Audit

Built in Google Slides. 100 points.

Standards Addressed

Nevada Academic Content Standards for Computer Science & Integrated Technology: 9-12.NI.NCO.1 (scalability/reliability of networks — routers, switches, servers, topology, addressing), 9-12.NI.C.1 (sensitive data affected by malware/attacks), 9-12.NI.C.2 (recommend security measures), 9-12.NI.C.3 (usability vs. security tradeoffs), 9-12.NI.C.4 (tradeoffs when implementing cybersecurity recommendations) — the same standards Chapter 17 (The Network Map), Chapter 18 (The Threat Log) and Chapter 19 (The Tradeoff Matrix, The Security Brief) build toward.

Objective

Audit a network — map its topology, identify real threats it faces, and recommend security measures with the tradeoffs explicitly weighed, not just "the most secure option."

Deliverable: A Google Slides deck, minimum 6 slides. Turn in a PDF export or a text file with your share link below.

New to Google Slides? Start here: Ch 4.0, The Office Suite, From Zero »

Steps

Each step below links to the chapter section that actually teaches how to do it — do that first if you haven't already.

  1. Diagram a real or realistic network (a home network, a small business, or a generalized school-style network — not exact real infrastructure) showing routers, switches, servers, and how devices are addressed. It must be a different network than the one you mapped in your Chapter 17 activity (your own home network) — this project is where you prove you can run the same process on something new, not where you write up work you already finished. How to: Ch 17.1, Routers, Switches & Topology »
  2. Identify 2+ real, specific threats this network is exposed to — not generic "hackers." How to: Ch 18.1, Malware Families » · Ch 18.3, Network Attacks & Real Defenses »
  3. For each threat, recommend a security measure, reasoning about efficiency and feasibility, not just picking the most secure option regardless of cost. How to: Ch 19.2, Writing a Recommendation Someone Will Fund »
  4. Compare 2 competing security measures head-to-head on usability vs. security tradeoffs. How to: Ch 19.1, The Usability »
  5. Final Recommendation — pick your overall approach and explicitly state what tradeoff you're accepting.
Worked Example: What a Finished Audit Looks Like

You may NOT use this network (a small bakery) for your own project. It's here so you can see how every slide connects to one network — pick your own and follow the same pattern.

The Network: A small family-owned bakery with free guest Wi-Fi for customers, one point-of-sale (POS) terminal, an office PC, and a security camera recorder (DVR). The owner is not a tech person and has a small budget.

Slide — Network Diagram

     │
 ISP modem
     │
 Router / firewall  (192.168.1.1)
     │
     ├── Staff network  192.168.1.0/24
     │     └── Switch
     │           ├── POS terminal      192.168.1.20
     │           ├── Office PC         192.168.1.21
     │           └── Camera DVR        192.168.1.30
     │
     └── Guest Wi-Fi    192.168.50.0/24  (customers' phones,
                                          addresses handed out
                                          automatically)

Private addresses only (192.168.x.x ranges are reserved for local networks), so this doesn't describe any real business.

Slide — Threats

  1. POS memory-scraping malware: Malware such as BlackPOS (used in the 2013 Target breach) reads card numbers out of a POS computer's memory in the split second before they're encrypted. If the office PC gets infected through a phishing email, the malware can spread to the POS because they're on the same network.
  2. Guest device reaching staff devices: If the guest Wi-Fi isn't actually isolated, any customer's phone or laptop on it can scan for and attack the POS and the DVR.
  3. DVR with a default password: Botnets such as Mirai (2016) took over huge numbers of cameras and DVRs simply by trying factory default usernames and passwords.

Slide — Recommendations

  • For threat 1: Ask the card processor for a standalone payment terminal with point-to-point encryption, so card numbers are encrypted inside the card reader and never sit readable on any bakery computer. Often low or no upfront cost from the processor, and the owner doesn't have to manage it.
  • For threat 2: Turn on the router's guest network isolation setting. Free, takes 10 minutes, and most small-business routers already have it.
  • For threat 3: Change the DVR's default password and block it from the internet. Free, one-time job; viewing cameras remotely is a nice-to-have, not a need.

Slide — Head-to-Head: Guest Wi-Fi

Option A: Isolated guest network, password on the counter signOption B: No guest Wi-Fi at all
SecurityGood. Guests can't see staff devices, but they still share the same router and internet connection.Best. No customer devices on the network at all.
UsabilityCustomers get Wi-Fi; password changes monthly and the sign gets updated.Customers who sit and work go elsewhere.
Cost to ownerFree; 5 minutes a month.Free, but may cost sales.

Slide — Final Recommendation

Keep guest Wi-Fi as Option A, move card payments to an encrypted standalone terminal, and lock down the DVR. Tradeoff accepted: guests still share the router with staff, so a flaw in the router itself could expose the staff network. I'm accepting that small risk because customers expect Wi-Fi, and the most valuable data (card numbers) is protected by the terminal no matter what happens on the network.
