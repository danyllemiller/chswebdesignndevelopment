"An algorithm is just math, so it can't be biased." Here's why that's wrong.

In this chapter: 11.1 Where Bias Actually Enters 11.2 Testing for Bias 11.3 Equity, Culture & Designing Responsibly

11.1 Where Bias Actually Enters

Math itself doesn't have opinions. But every algorithm is trained on data, built around a chosen goal, and designed by specific people — and bias slips in through all three, without anyone intending it.

Breaking It Down: What "Bias" Means in Computing

In everyday life, bias means leaning unfairly toward or against someone. In computing, algorithmic bias means a computer system gives unfair results to certain groups of people, again and again, in a pattern. For example, a system might work great for some people but make many more mistakes for others.

Here's the surprising part: a computer can be biased even when no one wanted it to be. The math inside a program doesn't have opinions. But programs learn from data collected from the real world, and they're built by people who make choices. If the data or the choices are lopsided, the program's results will be lopsided too.

Why does this matter so much? Because computers now help make big decisions: who gets a job interview, who gets approved for a loan, which patients get extra medical care. A biased person affects the people they meet. A biased program can affect millions of people at once, very quickly, and it can look "objective" while doing it.

The good news is that bias in computing is a problem you can find and fix, like any other bug. The rest of this chapter shows you the three places it usually sneaks in, and how to test for it on purpose.

11.1.1 Three Entry Points

The Training Data

If the data used to build a system doesn't represent everyone it will be used on, the system inherits that gap.

The Chosen Proxy

Systems can't measure "success" directly — they measure a stand-in for it. Pick the wrong stand-in and you optimize for the wrong thing entirely.

Who Was in the Room

A design team that all shares one background is likely to miss failure cases outside their own experience — not out of malice, just blind spots.

Breaking It Down: Entry Point 1: The Training Data

Many modern programs learn by studying examples. This collection of examples is called training data. A program that recognizes faces studies thousands of photos of faces. A program that understands speech listens to thousands of hours of people talking. Whatever is in those examples is what the program learns to be good at.

Now imagine the examples aren't balanced. If most of the face photos show light-skinned men, the program gets lots of practice with light-skinned men and very little practice with anyone else. This really happened. A 2018 MIT study called "Gender Shades," led by researcher Joy Buolamwini, tested commercial face-analysis systems. They were nearly perfect on lighter-skinned men but made far more mistakes on darker-skinned women.

Nobody programmed those systems to treat people differently. The gap came straight from the training data. That's why the first question to ask about any system that learns is: "Who is in the examples, and who is missing?"

Breaking It Down: Entry Point 2: The Chosen Proxy

Computers can only measure things that can be turned into numbers. But many things we care about, like "Is this student working hard?" or "Does this patient need extra care?", can't be measured directly. So designers pick a proxy: something measurable that stands in for the thing they really care about.

A proxy is like judging how hard a student studied by how many hours they sat at their desk. Sometimes that works, but someone could sit there for hours watching videos. If you pick the wrong proxy, your program carefully measures the wrong thing.

A real example: a 2019 study published in the journal Science examined a hospital algorithm that predicted which patients needed extra care. It used how much money had been spent on a patient's health care as a proxy for how sick they were. But because of unequal access to care, less money had historically been spent on Black patients who were just as sick. So the algorithm flagged them less often. The researchers showed that changing the proxy greatly reduced that bias.

Breaking It Down: Entry Point 3: Who Was in the Room

Every program is designed by a team of people, and every person has blind spots: things they don't notice because those things never happen to them. Someone who has never used a wheelchair might not notice a website that's impossible to use without a mouse. Someone whose name is always spelled "normally" by forms might not think about names with accents or hyphens.

When everyone on a design team shares the same background, they tend to share the same blind spots. They test the app the way they would use it, and it works. Then it reaches millions of people who are different from them, and problems show up that nobody on the team ever thought to check.

Voice assistants are a real example. A 2020 Stanford study found that major speech-recognition systems made almost twice as many errors with Black speakers as with white speakers. A team with a wider range of voices, accents, and life experiences is much more likely to catch problems like this before the product ships, not after.

Worked Example: Same Tool, Two Inputs

Run a real automated tool with two inputs that differ in exactly one attribute — say, a name commonly associated with different backgrounds — and compare the outputs side by side. When a single changed attribute shifts the result, that's not a coincidence. That's the tell that bias has entered somewhere upstream.

The trap: students often think bias requires someone to deliberately build a discriminatory system. Almost none of it works that way. Bias usually enters quietly, through a training set nobody double-checked or a proxy nobody questioned — which is exactly why testing for it has to be deliberate too.

11.2 Testing for Bias

Testing for bias isn't a philosophy debate — it's an engineering task with an actual method, the same way testing for any other bug is.

Breaking It Down: Bias Testing Is Bug Testing

Think back to Chapter 10, where you tested a function with a normal input, an empty input, and an invalid input. You didn't just hope it worked. You planned tests to prove it. Testing for bias works the same way. Instead of asking "Does it work?", you ask "Does it work equally well for different groups of people?"

The basic method is simple. Run the system with inputs that are the same in every way except one, such as a different name, a different accent, or a different neighborhood. Then compare the results side by side. If changing just that one thing changes the outcome, you've found a pattern worth investigating.

Real companies and researchers use the three steps listed below. None of them requires guessing anyone's intentions. They're just careful, honest testing, which makes them something any programmer can do.

11.2.1 The Mitigations

  • Diverse test data — deliberately test with inputs representing many different groups, not just the easiest ones to find.
  • Disaggregated results — report outcomes broken out by group instead of one averaged number that can hide unequal results.
  • An explicit review before release — a dedicated check for unequal handling, not an assumption that testing "in general" was enough.
Breaking It Down: Diverse Test Data

Diverse test data means testing your system with examples from many different kinds of people and situations, on purpose. It's easy to test with whatever is closest: your own name, your own voice, your friends' photos. But your friends probably have a lot in common with you, so they share your blind spots.

Imagine testing a new video game controller only on adults with large hands. It might feel perfect to them and be awkward for kids. To really test it, you'd need people with small hands, large hands, and people who play one-handed. Diverse test data is the same idea for software.

In practice, this means making a checklist of the groups your system will be used on and making sure each group shows up in your tests. If a group is missing from your test data, you have no idea how your system treats them.

Breaking It Down: Disaggregated Results

Disaggregated is a long word with a simple meaning: "broken apart into groups." Instead of reporting one overall number, you report the results separately for each group.

Here's why that matters. Suppose a face-unlock feature works 95% of the time overall. Sounds great! But break it apart, and you might find it works 99% of the time for one group and only 80% for another. The single average hid the problem. Both of these statements are true at once, but only the disaggregated one tells you who is being let down.

A good habit: whenever you see one big average, ask, "Is it the same for everyone?" Averages are useful, but they can hide real unfairness inside them.

Breaking It Down: An Explicit Review Before Release

An explicit review is a planned step, right before a product is released, where someone's whole job is to check for unequal treatment. It's like a final inspection before a car leaves the factory, but for fairness.

Why make it a separate step? Because when people are rushing to finish a project, "we tested it in general" can feel like enough. A dedicated review forces the team to stop and ask specific questions: Did we test with diverse data? Did we look at disaggregated results? Who might this fail, and what happens to them if it does?

Some companies have entire teams for this, and some governments are beginning to require these reviews for systems used in hiring, lending, and policing. Even on a small school project, you can do your own version: before you call it finished, write down who might be left out and how you checked.

Quick Check

Name two concrete ways to reduce bias in a system you're building. (Answer: testing with diverse data, and reporting results disaggregated by group instead of as one average.)

Activity: The Bias Test

If you have a prototype from an earlier chapter, use that. If not, pick a free online tool to test instead — a predictive-text keyboard, an autocomplete search bar, or a free AI image generator all work well.

  1. Like the Worked Example above, run your tool with at least 4 different inputs that vary in exactly one attribute at a time (different names, different phrasings, different assumed backgrounds).
  2. Record every output side by side, and identify at least one place where a single changed attribute produced a noticeably different result.
  3. Name where in the system that bias most likely entered from (the training data, a proxy variable, an assumption someone baked in).
  4. Propose and describe one concrete mitigation from 11.2.1 (diverse test data, disaggregated results, or an explicit review step) that would actually catch this specific bias if it were applied.

11.3 Equity, Culture & Designing Responsibly

Bias is one way computing can treat people unequally. It's not the only one — who gets to use technology at all, and who gets left out of building it, matter just as much.

11.3.1 The Equity Deficit (Digital Divide)

An equity deficit — often called the digital divide — is unequal access to computing resources: a rural community with no reliable broadband and few CS classes, compared to a nearby city with both. This gap shapes everything downstream — education, job access, even which voices get represented in the training data behind the algorithms discussed in 11.1. A developer building software for a global audience has to remember not every user has a new phone, fast internet, or an unlimited data plan.

Breaking It Down: The Digital Divide

The digital divide is the gap between people who have good access to technology and people who don't. "Access" includes having a working device, fast and reliable internet, and the skills and classes to use them well.

The divide shows up in many places. Some rural areas still don't have fast home internet, because running cables to spread-out homes is expensive. Some families share one phone among several people, or have a limited data plan that runs out by the middle of the month. When schools switched to online learning in 2020, many students couldn't join video classes from home. This problem is often called the "homework gap."

This gap has ripple effects. Students with less access get less practice with technology, which can mean fewer tech classes and fewer tech jobs later. And because those communities are online less, they show up less in the training data from 11.1, which means the systems built from that data may work worse for them. The divide feeds itself.

As a builder, you can help close the gap with simple choices: make your website work on older phones, keep images small so they load on slow connections, and don't assume every user has unlimited data or a brand-new device.

11.3.2 Computational Artifacts & Accessibility

Anything built with code — an app, a website, a program — is a computational artifact. Making one usable by everyone means designing in features like text-to-speech and adjustable font sizes from the start, not bolting them on afterward. Accessibility is a requirement, not an extra: an artifact that only works for users without disabilities has failed part of its actual audience.

Breaking It Down: Designing for Everyone from the Start

Around 1 in 4 adults in the United States lives with some kind of disability, according to the CDC. That includes people who are blind or have low vision, people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people who have trouble using their hands, and people with learning or attention differences. If your app doesn't work for them, it doesn't work for a huge part of its audience.

Many accessibility features are simple. Text-to-speech reads the screen out loud. Adjustable font sizes let people zoom in without breaking the layout. Captions make videos usable for people who can't hear them, and for anyone watching in a noisy cafeteria. Alt text describes images for screen readers.

The key phrase in this section is "from the start." Adding accessibility at the very end is like trying to add a wheelchair ramp to a building after it's finished. It's possible, but it's harder, more expensive, and usually worse. When you plan accessibility from the first sketch, it becomes part of the design instead of a patch. Bonus: features like captions and bigger buttons end up helping almost everyone.

Why Diverse Teams Build Better Artifacts

A team built entirely of people who share one background is statistically likely to share the same blind spots (see 11.1.1's "Who Was in the Room"). Companies intentionally build development teams from different backgrounds specifically because a wider range of lived experience catches failure cases — and unfair defaults — a more uniform team would miss entirely.

11.3.3 Computing's Cultural & Economic Ripple Effects

A computational innovation doesn't just add a new tool — it can reshape a cultural practice (how people meet, shop, or get news) and reshape the job market at the same time (new roles created, others automated away). Before shipping something new, a responsible team thinks ahead about how it could be misused — a social app's addictive design, or how easily it could spread misinformation — not just how it's meant to be used.

Breaking It Down: Cultural & Economic Ripple Effects

When a new technology spreads, it rarely changes just one thing. It sends out ripples, like a stone dropped in a pond. Think about smartphones. They changed how we take photos, but they also changed how people meet, how news spreads, how we shop, and even how we give directions ("just send me the location").

A cultural change is a change in people's habits and shared expectations. For example, many people now expect an instant reply to a text. An economic change is a change in money and jobs. Streaming music created new jobs in data and app design, while CD stores mostly disappeared. Usually both kinds of change happen at the same time.

Some ripples are good, and some are harmful. That's why the next idea, thinking ahead about misuse, is part of building responsibly.

Breaking It Down: Thinking Ahead About Misuse

Every tool can be used in ways its designers never intended. A messaging app built to keep friends in touch can also be used to bully someone. A video app built for fun can be designed so it's very hard to put down. A tool that makes sharing easy also makes sharing false information easy.

Responsible teams ask "How could this go wrong?" before they release a product, not after the headlines. Some teams even run a planning exercise where they pretend to be a bad actor and try to misuse their own app, so they can block the worst problems early.

Practical fixes often come from design choices. Examples include limits on how fast a message can be forwarded to many people, easy ways to report and block, and reminders to take a break. You'll practice spotting misuse and designing a fix in the Global Audience Redesign activity below.
