Unit 5: Impacts of Computing

Chapter 11: Culture, Equity & Bias

"An algorithm is just math, so it can't be biased." Here's why that's wrong.

In this chapter: 11.1 Where Bias Actually Enters 11.2 Testing for Bias 11.3 Equity, Culture & Designing Responsibly

11.1 Where Bias Actually Enters

Math itself doesn't have opinions. But every algorithm is trained on data, built around a chosen goal, and designed by specific people — and bias slips in through all three, without anyone intending it.

11.1.1 Three Entry Points

The Training Data

If the data used to build a system doesn't represent everyone it will be used on, the system inherits that gap.

The Chosen Proxy

Systems can't measure "success" directly — they measure a stand-in for it. Pick the wrong stand-in and you optimize for the wrong thing entirely.

Who Was in the Room

A design team that all shares one background is likely to miss failure cases outside their own experience — not out of malice, just blind spots.

Worked Example: Same Tool, Two Inputs

Run a real automated tool with two inputs that differ in exactly one attribute — say, a name commonly associated with different backgrounds — and compare the outputs side by side. When a single changed attribute shifts the result, that's not a coincidence. That's the tell that bias has entered somewhere upstream.

The trap: students often think bias requires someone to deliberately build a discriminatory system. Almost none of it works that way. Bias usually enters quietly, through a training set nobody double-checked or a proxy nobody questioned — which is exactly why testing for it has to be deliberate too.

11.2 Testing for Bias

Testing for bias isn't a philosophy debate — it's an engineering task with an actual method, the same way testing for any other bug is.

11.2.1 The Mitigations

  • Diverse test data — deliberately test with inputs representing many different groups, not just the easiest ones to find.
  • Disaggregated results — report outcomes broken out by group instead of one averaged number that can hide unequal results.
  • An explicit review before release — a dedicated check for unequal handling, not an assumption that testing "in general" was enough.
Quick Check

Name two concrete ways to reduce bias in a system you're building. (Answer: testing with diverse data, and reporting results disaggregated by group instead of as one average.)

Activity: The Bias Test

If you have a prototype from an earlier chapter, use that. If not, pick a free online tool to test instead — a predictive-text keyboard, an autocomplete search bar, or a free AI image generator all work well.

  1. Like the Worked Example above, run your tool with at least 4 different inputs that vary in exactly one attribute at a time (different names, different phrasings, different assumed backgrounds).
  2. Record every output side by side, and identify at least one place where a single changed attribute produced a noticeably different result.
  3. Name where in the system that bias most likely entered from (the training data, a proxy variable, an assumption someone baked in).
  4. Propose and describe one concrete mitigation from 11.2.1 (diverse test data, disaggregated results, or an explicit review step) that would actually catch this specific bias if it were applied.

Turn In: Your tool and inputs, your side-by-side outputs, where you think the bias entered, and your proposed mitigation.

11.3 Equity, Culture & Designing Responsibly

Bias is one way computing can treat people unequally. It's not the only one — who gets to use technology at all, and who gets left out of building it, matter just as much.

11.3.1 The Equity Deficit (Digital Divide)

An equity deficit — often called the digital divide — is unequal access to computing resources: a rural community with no reliable broadband and few CS classes, compared to a nearby city with both. This gap shapes everything downstream — education, job access, even which voices get represented in the training data behind the algorithms discussed in 11.1. A developer building software for a global audience has to remember not every user has a new phone, fast internet, or an unlimited data plan.

11.3.2 Computational Artifacts & Accessibility

Anything built with code — an app, a website, a program — is a computational artifact. Making one usable by everyone means designing in features like text-to-speech and adjustable font sizes from the start, not bolting them on afterward. Accessibility is a requirement, not an extra: an artifact that only works for users without disabilities has failed part of its actual audience.

Why Diverse Teams Build Better Artifacts

A team built entirely of people who share one background is statistically likely to share the same blind spots (see 11.1.1's "Who Was in the Room"). Companies intentionally build development teams from different backgrounds specifically because a wider range of lived experience catches failure cases — and unfair defaults — a more uniform team would miss entirely.

11.3.3 Computing's Cultural & Economic Ripple Effects

A computational innovation doesn't just add a new tool — it can reshape a cultural practice (how people meet, shop, or get news) and reshape the job market at the same time (new roles created, others automated away). Before shipping something new, a responsible team thinks ahead about how it could be misused — a social app's addictive design, or how easily it could spread misinformation — not just how it's meant to be used.

Quick Check

What term describes unequal access to computing resources between two communities? (Answer: an equity deficit, or digital divide.)

Activity: The Global Audience Redesign

Pick any app or website you use regularly.

  1. List two ways it would fail (or frustrate) a user with an older phone, a slow or limited data connection, or a disability affecting sight, hearing, or motor control.
  2. Propose one specific accessibility fix for one of those failures (e.g. adding text-to-speech, reducing data/image size, larger tap targets).
  3. Describe one way this exact app could realistically be misused (addictive design patterns, spreading misinformation, enabling harassment), and one design change that would reduce that risk.
  4. Name one real cultural or economic shift this kind of app has caused — a habit it changed, or a job market it reshaped.

Turn In: Your two accessibility failures and fix, your misuse scenario and mitigation, and your cultural/economic impact example.
