16.1 The Lifecycle & Real User Feedback

Building exactly what someone asked for and building something they'll actually use are two different outcomes — and the gap between them is almost always feedback nobody collected.

16.1.1 Six Stages That Loop, Not End

Analyse
Design
Develop
Test
Deploy
Maintain

Six stages, and the arrow doesn't stop at Maintain — real feedback loops back around to Analyse, starting the cycle again. Feedback isn't a courtesy tacked onto the end. It's a required stage.

Worked Example: Tracing Feedback to a Decision

A tester says "I couldn't tell if my submission actually worked." Trace that back: the design decision was skipping a confirmation message after submission. Document the change — add a confirmation — and you've closed the loop from real feedback to a specific fix, instead of guessing at improvements nobody asked for.

The trap: students often think every piece of feedback should be implemented. It shouldn't — declining feedback with a stated reason is a legitimate design decision, as long as you can defend why.

16.1.2 Documenting Design Decisions

An undocumented decision gets reversed by the next person who works on it, simply because they don't know why it was made that way in the first place. A short design log — decision plus reasoning — prevents that.

Quick Check

Why document a design decision instead of just making the change? (Answer: an undocumented decision gets reversed later by someone who doesn't know why it was made that way.)

Activity: The Feedback Cycle

Test your tool with two people outside this class and log every point of confusion. Choose two pieces of feedback to act on and two to decline — with stated reasons — and document each design decision in a short log.

16.2 Licensing & Intellectual Property

You find a code library online that does exactly what you need. Free to download doesn't automatically mean free to use — check the license before you build anything on top of it.

16.2.1 Three License Families

Permissive

Use it almost anywhere, with minimal obligations.

Copyleft

Anything you build on top must be released under that same license.

Proprietary

Owned outright, use is tightly restricted by the owner's terms.

Both sides of this are worth knowing honestly: IP law funds creators and protects real investment — and it can also block follow-on work that would have made something even better. Neither side is the whole truth.

Activity: The License Audit

Audit three real libraries or assets and record each license and what it requires of you. Write correct attribution for every third-party element in your Unit 4 tool.

16.3 Accessibility

Try operating a real interface with only the keyboard, no mouse. The failures show up immediately — and most of them are fixable in minutes once you know to look for them.

Worked Example: A Situational Disability

A "situational disability" is a temporary condition creating the exact same barrier as a permanent one — bright sunlight washing out low-contrast text, or a broken arm making a mouse impossible to use for a few weeks. Fixing for permanent disabilities helps everyone dealing with a temporary version of the same barrier.

Concrete, low-effort fixes: alt text on images and charts, sufficient color contrast, meaningful sheet and range names instead of "Sheet1," fully keyboard-reachable controls, and plain-language instructions.

The trap: students often treat accessibility as an add-on step at the very end. Evaluate and refine — it's a genuine revision pass on an existing artifact, not a checkbox added after the "real" work is done.

Quick Check

What's a situational disability? Give an example. (Answer: a temporary condition creating the same barrier as a permanent disability — like bright sun making a screen hard to read, or a broken arm making a mouse hard to use.)

Activity: The Accessibility Pass

Audit your Unit 4 tool against all five barrier types and log every failure. Fix at least four, including alt text and contrast, then operate the whole tool without a mouse and document what still doesn't work.
