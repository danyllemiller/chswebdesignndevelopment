What's the difference between a chart of what happened and a model of what will happen next?

In this chapter: 8.1 Anatomy of a Model 8.2 Sensitivity Testing 8.3 Validating Against Reality 8.4 Simulations & Complex Systems

8.1 Anatomy of a Model

A chart describes the past. A computational model makes a claim about the future — and unlike a guess, that claim is testable, because it's built from explicit rules you can check.

8.1.1 Four Required Parts

Inputs

The starting values you plug in — the numbers you can actually change.

Required because: without something you can actually change, there's nothing left to test — you'd just have one fixed number forever.

Relationships

Formulas describing how the inputs affect each other and the outcome.

Required because: inputs alone don't predict anything — the relationship is the actual claim being made about how the world works.

Outputs

What the model actually predicts, given the current inputs.

Required because: a model that doesn't produce something testable isn't a model — it's just an opinion.

Assumptions

Everything you're taking for granted, stated out loud instead of hidden.

Required because: an unstated assumption can't be questioned or tested — writing it down is what turns a silent guess into something someone else can actually check.

Worked Example: A Compound-Interest Model

Inputs: starting balance, interest rate, number of years. Relationship: a formula multiplying the balance by the rate each year and adding it back in. Output: the final balance. Assumption: the interest rate never changes — which is exactly the part worth questioning.

The trap: students often think a model with more inputs is automatically more accurate. It isn't, if the relationships are wrong or an assumption is unrealistic. A simple model with honest, stated assumptions beats a complicated one hiding a bad guess.

8.2 Sensitivity Testing

Building a model isn't the finish line. The next question is: which of your assumptions is the model actually resting its whole prediction on?

8.2.1 Change One Input, Watch the Output

Sensitivity testing means changing exactly one input at a time and recording how much the output moves. The input that swings the output the most is the one your model is most dependent on — and the one most worth questioning before you trust the result.

TestStarting BalanceRateYearsOutputSwing from Baseline
Baseline$1,0005%10$1,628.89—
Rate → 8%$1,0008%10$2,158.92+32.5%
Years → 15$1,0005%15$2,078.93+27.6%
Balance → $2,000$2,0005%10$3,257.79+100.0% (exactly matches the doubled input)

Change the rate alone, and the 10-year output swings 32.5%. Change years alone, it swings 27.6%. This model's output is more sensitive to the interest rate than to how long the money sits — that's exactly the kind of thing sensitivity testing is built to reveal.

Worked Example: Breaking a Model on Purpose

Enter an absurd input into your own model — a negative population, an interest rate of 500% — and watch what the output does. If it produces a nonsense number without complaint, that's not a bug in the spreadsheet. That's your model telling you it was never validated against unrealistic input in the first place.

The trap: students often think a model that runs without errors must be correct. Running and being correct are not the same thing — a model happily calculates a confident, wrong answer if you never test it against edge cases and absurd inputs.

Quick Check

What does sensitivity testing actually tell you? (Answer: which input your model's outcome depends on most — found by changing one input at a time and observing the output.)

Activity: The Model Build

Pick something you can predict with a formula — how much you'd save skipping a daily coffee run, how a phone's battery drains over a day of use, how long a road trip takes at different speeds. If you're stuck, model something from your own life you're actually curious about.

  1. In a spreadsheet, build your model with at least three inputs and one formula that calculates an outcome from them.
  2. Write out every assumption your model is quietly relying on — like the compound-interest example's "the rate never changes."
  3. Run a sensitivity test: change one input at a time, record how much the output moves each time, and identify which input your model depends on most.
  4. Name one real-world thing your model can't account for — something it ignores that would actually matter.

8.3 Validating Against Reality

Surviving a sensitivity test still doesn't prove a model is good — it only proves you know which input it's most sensitive to. The real test is older and simpler: does the model's prediction match what actually happens?

8.3.1 Fitting the Past vs. Predicting the Future

Here's the trap hiding inside every model: it's always possible to tune a model until it matches historical data perfectly. That's not the same accomplishment as building a model that correctly predicts something it's never seen. A model that's been adjusted until it explains every wiggle in the past — instead of the general trend — has usually just memorized noise, not learned the real relationship.

Worked Example: The Forecast That Only Works Backward

A student builds a formula that reproduces last month's daily temperatures with almost no error — by adding a separate correction term for nearly every single day. It looks incredible on a chart of the past. Fed tomorrow's conditions, it's no better than a random guess, because it never learned what actually drives temperature — it just memorized last month's answer key.

DayActual TempModel PredictedError
Training DataMar 158°F58°F0°
Mar 261°F61°F0°
Mar 355°F55°F0°
New DataMar 3164°F41°F23°

Perfect on every training day — because it had a custom-fitted term for each one — and off by 23 degrees the first time it had to predict a day it hadn't already memorized the answer to.

The trap: students often assume the model that matches historical data most closely is automatically the best one. Often it's the opposite — a model chasing every past data point instead of the underlying pattern tends to be the one that fails hardest on anything new.

Breaking It Down: What "Validity" Means for a Model

A model's validity is how well it matches the real thing it's supposed to represent. A valid model gives results close to what actually happens. An invalid model can look impressive and still give answers that don't match reality.

The main way to check validity is to test the model against real data you already have. Say you built a model of how a flu spreads through a school. You'd run it using last year's starting numbers, then compare its prediction to what really happened last year. If the two line up closely, that's strong evidence the model is valid.

Validation isn't a one-time stamp of approval. Real conditions change, so good modelers keep checking their models against new data. When the model and reality start to disagree, that's the signal to fix the model's rules or inputs.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Checking a Model Against Real Data

Running a disease model on last year's starting numbers and comparing its prediction to what really happened tests the model's validity: how closely it matches reality. How fast the simulation runs has nothing to do with validity.

Breaking It Down: Overfitting

Overfitting is when a model learns the past too well. It memorizes every random bump in the old data instead of learning the general pattern, so it falls apart on anything new.

It's like studying for a test by memorizing the exact answers to the practice test. If the real test has the same questions, you ace it. If the questions are even slightly different, you're lost, because you never learned how to solve them. An overfit model has memorized the practice test.

How do you catch overfitting? Hold some data back. Build your model using most of your data, then test it on data it has never seen, like the last month you held back. If it does great on the old data but badly on the new data, it's overfit. A simpler model that captures the general trend usually predicts the future better.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: The Real Danger of Overfitting

An overfit model matches the past almost perfectly but fails to predict anything new, because it memorized the old data instead of learning the pattern. The danger isn't too many charts on a dashboard. It is a model that only works backward.

Quick Check

A model explains ten years of past data almost perfectly, then performs badly predicting year eleven. What's the most likely problem? (Answer: overfitting — the model was tuned to match the noise in the past data instead of the real underlying relationship.)

8.4 Simulations & Complex Systems

The compound-interest model earlier in this chapter calculates one final answer from a formula. Some real-world systems are too tangled for that — they need something that runs forward through time instead.

8.4.1 Complex Systems

A system counts as genuinely complex when it has many interacting parts whose combined behavior is hard to predict just by looking at each part alone — a wildfire's spread depends on wind, humidity, terrain, and fuel all interacting at once; traffic flow depends on hundreds of individual drivers reacting to each other in real time. You can't just plug numbers into one formula and get a trustworthy answer the way you could for a savings account.

8.4.2 Simulation vs. a Static Model

A static model (like this chapter's compound-interest example) calculates one output from one set of inputs. A simulation runs that process forward, often step by step through time or over many repeated trials, to see how a complex system behaves — a meteorologist's storm-tracking software doesn't solve one equation, it simulates the storm's likely path minute by minute based on current conditions and physics.

Static Model — one formula, one answer

Input20 bacteria, doubling every hour
Formula=20 * 2^6
Output1,280 after 6 hours

Simulation — the same system, walked forward hour by hour

Population2040801603206401,280

Both land on the same final number — but the simulation shows the population at every hour along the way, not just the ending. That's what lets a simulation catch something a static formula can't: what if food ran out at hour 4, before the population ever reached 1,280?

Why Simulations Use Random Numbers

Real-world systems don't behave identically every time — customers don't arrive at a store on a perfect schedule. Simulations often add a random number generator to introduce that same realistic variability (e.g. randomizing exactly when each simulated customer arrives), instead of pretending everything happens like clockwork.

Breaking It Down: Why Simulations Use Random Numbers

Real life is full of chance. A store might know that about 30 customers come in each hour, but it can't know exactly when each one will walk through the door. Some minutes are packed and some are empty.

A model that ignored chance would treat every hour exactly the same, and that's not how the world works. A random-number generator lets the simulation copy that natural unpredictability. One run might have a rush at 12:05, another at 12:40, just like real days do.

Because each run comes out a little different, modelers run a simulation many times and look at the overall pattern, like the average wait time and the worst case. That gives a much more honest picture than a single run that pretends nothing is ever left to chance.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Random Numbers Copy Real-Life Chance

Simulations add a random number generator to copy the natural unpredictability of real events, like customers arriving at slightly different times each day. It has nothing to do with hardware, privacy, or network addresses. It just keeps the model from pretending everything happens like clockwork.

Breaking It Down: Randomness in a Spreadsheet Simulation

Google Sheets has built-in random number generators. =RAND() gives a random decimal between 0 and 1. =RANDBETWEEN(1, 6) gives a random whole number from 1 to 6, like rolling a die. Every time the sheet recalculates, these numbers change.

You can use them to add realistic variety to a model. Say a store usually gets 8 to 12 customers an hour. Instead of assuming exactly 10 every hour, a simulation could use =RANDBETWEEN(8, 12) for each hour. Now each run of the simulation plays out a little differently, just like real life.

Because one random run could be lucky or unlucky, simulations are run many times, and the results are averaged. Run a dice simulation 10 times and the average might be off. Run it 1,000 times and the average gets very close to the true answer of 3.5. More trials mean more trustworthy results.

8.4.3 Abstraction: Simplifying on Purpose

A climate model could try to track every single molecule of air on Earth — but that's not practical, and it wouldn't even help. Abstraction means deliberately leaving out details that don't matter for the question you're actually asking, so the model stays understandable and runs in a reasonable amount of time. A traffic simulation that ignores each driver's personality and just models speed, spacing, and traffic lights isn't lazy — it's abstracted to the level of detail that traffic flow actually depends on.

Breaking It Down: Abstraction in Models

Abstraction means keeping the details that matter for your question and leaving out the rest. A subway map is a great example. It doesn't show streets, buildings, or the real distances between stations. It shows only the stations and the lines connecting them, because that's all a rider needs.

Every model is an abstraction. A model of how fast a rumor spreads at school might track how many people each person talks to per day. It doesn't need anyone's height, favorite food, or locker number. Leaving those out isn't lazy. It makes the model faster to build, easier to understand, and easier to check.

The skill is choosing the right details. Leave out something that really matters, like the fact that weekends exist and people talk less, and your model gives wrong answers. A good test: for each detail you leave out, ask "Would including this change the answer to my question?" If yes, keep it.

Quick Check

A climate model leaves out the behavior of every individual air molecule. Why is that a reasonable tradeoff instead of a flaw? (Answer: abstraction — tracking that much detail wouldn't be practical, and the model doesn't need it to answer the question being asked.)

8.4.4 Hypotheses: What Simulations Are Really Testing

A hypothesis is a specific, testable claim — "if we increase advertising spend by 20%, sales will rise by 5%." A model or simulation is one of the main tools for testing a hypothesis without waiting for the real thing to happen: run the simulation, compare its prediction to what actually occurs, and either the hypothesis holds up or it gets refined based on where the model's output and reality disagreed.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: What a Hypothesis Is, and What Models Are For
  • "If we increase advertising spend by 20%, sales will rise by 5%" is a hypothesis: a specific claim you are about to test. It is not proven, and it is not an algorithm.
  • Models are best for complex situations that change over time and are hard or dangerous to test for real, like a disease spreading through a city for 30 days. Compressing a video to email it is not modeling.
Breaking It Down: Writing a Testable Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a specific prediction you can check, usually written as "If I change ___, then ___ will happen." For example: "If the cafeteria opens a second lunch line, then the average wait time will drop from 12 minutes to under 7."

Good hypotheses are specific and measurable. "A second line will make lunch better" can't really be tested. What counts as "better"? But "Average wait time will drop under 7 minutes" can be tested. Run the simulation, measure the wait, and see if you were right.

Being wrong isn't a failure. If the simulation shows wait time only drops to 10 minutes, you've learned something real. Maybe the bottleneck is the cash register, not the line. Scientists and engineers improve their thinking by testing hypotheses, seeing where they're wrong, and refining them.
