1.1 Your Digital Identity

Tonight, somewhere, an employer, a college admissions officer, or a coach is going to search your name. What comes up is not random — it's the sum of every account you've made, every post you've left up, and every photo someone else tagged you in. That sum has a name: your digital identity.

1.1.1 The Footprint You Leave

Your digital footprint has two halves, and most people only think about one of them.

Active Footprint

Everything you deliberately publish: posts, comments, usernames, profile photos, the videos you make. You chose to put this here.

Passive Footprint

Everything collected or posted about you without a deliberate choice on your part: photos friends tag you in, search history, location data, an old account you forgot existed.

Reputation isn't built by one post — it's built by accumulation. A single awkward comment from three years ago won't sink you. A pattern of them will. That's why "I'll delete it later" is a weaker plan than "I'll think about it before I post it."

Worked Example: One Post, Three Audiences

Say you post: "This assignment is so dumb, waste of my life." Read it as three different people:

  • A friend reads it as venting. No big deal.
  • A teacher reads it as disrespect for their class, even if that's not what you meant.
  • A hiring manager, scrolling your public profile before an interview, reads it as a preview of how you'll talk about their company.

Same seven words. Three completely different verdicts. That gap is the entire reason digital identity matters.

1.1.2 Choosing a Professional Identity

Every account you create is an identity decision, starting with the username. A username like xXcrazygamer420Xx is fine for a private gaming account. It is not fine on something that will appear next to your real submitted work for the rest of the year — and possibly on a resume link someday.

Gamertag vs. Professional Handle

[AI Prompt]: A clean split-image graphic. Left side shows a flashy, neon gamertag with a skull emoji. Right side shows a plain, lowercase professional username like "jsmith26" next to a simple resume icon.

The trap: students often think a professional identity has to be boring or fake. It doesn't. It just has to be something you'd be fine explaining to an employer. "amiller24" isn't a lie — it's just the version of your name a stranger can trust.

Quick Check

A friend tags you in an unflattering photo you never posted yourself. Is that part of your active footprint or your passive footprint? (Answer: passive — you didn't choose to publish it, but it's still part of what a stranger can find.)

1.2 Safe, Legal & Ethical Habits Online

Knowing what you can do online and knowing what you should do online are two different skills. This section is about the second one — and about speaking up when someone else gets it wrong.

1.2.1 Four Words That Sound the Same But Aren't

People use "safe," "legal," "ethical," and "positive" interchangeably. They shouldn't — each one protects something different.

Safe

Protects you — your accounts, your data, your reputation.

Legal

Protects you from consequences — fines, suspension, a record.

Ethical

Protects other people, even when nobody's watching.

Positive

Actually builds something instead of just avoiding harm.

Something can be legal and still unethical. Screenshotting a friend's private message and sharing it isn't usually illegal — but it's a betrayal, and everyone in the room knows it. Being a good digital citizen means clearing all four bars, not just the one with a punishment attached.

1.2.2 The Bystander Problem

Most online harm doesn't continue because attackers are unstoppable. It continues because witnesses say nothing. Advocating for good behavior — not just practicing it yourself — is the harder half of digital citizenship, and it's a skill you can actually train.

Two Scripts for the Same Situation

Scenario: A group chat starts piling on one person over a mistake they made.

Escalates: "Yeah honestly they kind of deserve it lol" — even a joking pile-on adds fuel.

De-escalates: "Okay I think that's enough, let's move on" — short, doesn't lecture anyone, and it works because someone finally said it.

The trap: students often think intervening means a big dramatic confrontation. It almost never does. The de-escalating line above is eleven words. Most of the time that's all it takes — nobody else wanted to be the one to say it.

1.3 Recognizing Online Dangers

Cybersecurity has a reputation problem: people picture a hacker in a hoodie breaking encryption. In reality, almost nobody breaks encryption. They just ask you nicely for your password — and dress the question up so it doesn't look like a question.

1.3.1 The Human Attacks

These attacks don't target your computer. They target you.

The Fake Message

An email or text pretending to be someone official — your school, a bank, a game you play — asking you to click a link and log in.

The tells: a sender domain that's almost right but not quite (micros0ft-support.com), manufactured urgency ("your account will be deleted in 24 hours!"), and a link whose visible text doesn't match where it actually goes.

Someone Pretending to Be Someone Else

An account copying a real person's name and photo — a teacher, a friend, a celebrity — to trick you into trusting a message you'd normally ignore.

The tells: a brand-new account, a slightly misspelled username, and a request that a real version of that person would never actually make over DM (like asking for money or a password).

Reusing a Leaked Password

Attackers don't guess your password — they buy a list of real ones leaked from some other site's breach, then try that exact email-and-password combo everywhere else.

Why it works: most people reuse the same password across many sites. One breach anywhere becomes a master key everywhere, unless every password is unique.

1.3.2 Prevention and Detection

You can't out-guess every attack, but you can make yourself an expensive target instead of an easy one.

The Habits That Actually Work
  • A unique password per site, kept in a password manager instead of your memory.
  • Multi-factor authentication — a second code from your phone, so a stolen password alone isn't enough.
  • Verifying out of band — if a "school" text asks you to log in urgently, don't click the link. Open the school's site yourself, the way you normally would.

The trap: students often think that if they didn't get "hacked" in some dramatic movie sense, they weren't affected by a breach. In reality, most breaches are silent — a company's database leaks, your old password from three years ago is now on a public list, and you never notice until someone tries it on your current accounts.

If You Think You Already Clicked Something
  1. Change the password for that account immediately, from a different, trusted device.
  2. Turn on multi-factor authentication if it isn't already on.
  3. Check anywhere else you used that same password — and change those too.
