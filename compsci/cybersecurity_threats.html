18.1 Malware Families

"Malware" isn't one thing — it's a category covering software with very different jobs, from quietly spying on you to actively holding your files hostage.

18.1.1 Five Families, Five Different Goals

TypeWhat It Actually Does
VirusAttaches itself to files and spreads when they're shared.
WormSpreads on its own across a network, no file-sharing required.
TrojanDisguises itself as legitimate software to get installed.
RansomwareEncrypts your files and extorts payment to unlock them.
SpywareCollects your data secretly, without your knowledge.
Worked Example: How Ransomware Actually Costs You

Ransomware doesn't steal your files in the usual sense — it encrypts the records already sitting on your own drive, so you can no longer access your own data without paying for the key. That's a concrete example of how malware affects sensitive data: it doesn't have to leave your device to cause real damage.

18.2 Human Attacks

Cybersecurity has a reputation for being about clever code exploits. In reality, the majority of successful attacks target people, not systems — because it's almost always easier to trick someone than to break encryption.

18.2.1 Phishing & Its Tells

Phishing is an attack that tricks a person into surrendering credentials or data through a fake message pretending to be legitimate. The tells are consistent once you know to look for them:

  • A sender domain that's almost right but not quite.
  • Manufactured urgency — "your account will be deleted in 24 hours."
  • A link whose visible text doesn't match where it actually leads.
Impersonation & Credential Stuffing

Impersonation: an account copying someone real to borrow trust you wouldn't extend to a stranger.

Credential stuffing: reusing a password leaked from one breach and trying that exact combination everywhere else — which is exactly why reusing passwords is so dangerous, even on accounts that feel unimportant.

The trap: students often think cybersecurity is someone else's job — the IT department's. It's everyone's responsibility, because the weakest point in almost any system is a person who clicks the wrong link, not a flaw in the encryption itself.

Quick Check

Why is cybersecurity described as everyone's responsibility, not just the IT department's? (Answer: most successful attacks target people through tricks like phishing, not the underlying systems themselves.)
