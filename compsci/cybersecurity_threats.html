Chapter 18: Cybersecurity Threats

What's the most common way an attacker actually gets into an account — a clever exploit, or something else entirely?

In this chapter: 18.1 Malware Families 18.2 Human Attacks 18.3 Network Attacks & Real Defenses

18.1 Malware Families

"Malware" isn't one thing — it's a category covering software with very different jobs, from quietly spying on you to actively holding your files hostage.

Breaking It Down: What Malware Is

Malware is short for "malicious software." That means any program built to harm a device, steal information, or take control without permission. It's a big umbrella word, like "illness." A cold and a broken leg are both health problems, but they're very different. In the same way, a virus and spyware are both malware, but they work very differently.

Malware usually gets in through everyday actions. Someone opens an email attachment. Someone downloads a "free" game from a sketchy website. Someone clicks a pop-up or plugs in a strange USB drive. Most malware needs a person to make one small mistake to get started.

Knowing the different families matters because each one has a different goal and needs a different defense. Spyware wants to stay hidden, so it's quiet. Ransomware wants to be noticed, so it announces itself with a ransom note. The five families below cover the most common types.

18.1.1 Five Families, Five Different Goals

TypeWhat It Actually Does
VirusAttaches itself to files and spreads when they're shared.
WormSpreads on its own across a network, no file-sharing required.
TrojanDisguises itself as legitimate software to get installed.
RansomwareEncrypts your files and extorts payment to unlock them.
SpywareCollects your data secretly, without your knowledge.
Breaking It Down: Viruses

A computer virus works a lot like a cold virus. It attaches itself to something else, usually a file or program, and spreads when that file is shared and opened. Until someone opens or runs the infected file, the virus just sits there.

Once it runs, a virus can copy itself into other files on the same computer and do whatever harm it was built for, like deleting files, slowing the computer, or opening the door for other malware. Then, when you share an infected file with a friend, the cycle starts again on their device.

Defense: don't open files from people or sites you don't trust, even if they look harmless, and keep antivirus software turned on and up to date. Antivirus works by recognizing known viruses and blocking them before they run.

Breaking It Down: Worms

A worm spreads by itself. Unlike a virus, it doesn't need anyone to open a file. It finds weaknesses in other computers on the network and copies itself onto them automatically, then those computers do the same.

That's what makes worms so fast. One infected computer can infect ten, those ten can infect a hundred, and within hours a worm can spread around the world. In 2017, the WannaCry attack used a worm to spread ransomware to more than 200,000 computers in about 150 countries, including hospitals in the United Kingdom.

Defense: install software updates quickly. Worms usually spread through security holes that the software maker has already fixed. The computers that get hit are the ones that never installed the fix.

Breaking It Down: Trojans

A Trojan (or Trojan horse) is named after an ancient Greek story. The Greeks hid soldiers inside a giant wooden horse, gave it to the city of Troy as a "gift," and the soldiers climbed out at night. A Trojan program pretends to be something useful, like a free game, a video player, or a "speed booster," but hides harmful code inside.

The key thing about Trojans is that you install them yourself, because you think they're legitimate. Once installed, a Trojan might steal passwords, spy on what you type, or quietly let an attacker control your computer from far away.

Defense: only download apps from official stores and trusted websites. Be very suspicious of anything "free" that's normally expensive, and of pop-ups saying "Your computer is infected, download this to fix it." That message itself is often the Trojan.

Breaking It Down: Ransomware

Ransomware locks up your files by encrypting them: scrambling them with a secret key so they become unreadable. Then it shows a message demanding payment, usually in cryptocurrency, in exchange for the key to unlock them.

Ransomware attacks have hit schools, hospitals, city governments, and businesses, shutting them down for days or weeks. Paying doesn't guarantee you'll get your files back. Criminals don't always keep their promises, and paying encourages more attacks.

The best defense is backups: extra copies of your important files stored somewhere the ransomware can't reach. If your files get locked, you can erase the infected computer and restore everything from the backup without paying anything. Chapter 19 explains how to set backups up correctly.

Breaking It Down: Spyware

Spyware secretly watches what you do and sends it to someone else. It might record the websites you visit. It might capture what you type, including passwords. It can track where you go, or even turn on your camera or microphone.

Spyware is built to hide, so most people never notice it. Watch for warning signs. Your device might run slow or hot for no reason. Your battery might drain fast. You might see strange pop-ups or settings you didn't change. Some spyware, called "stalkerware," is put on a phone by someone the owner knows.

Defense: lock your phone with a passcode only you know. Check which apps can use your location, camera, and microphone. Delete any app you don't know or don't use.

Worked Example: How Ransomware Actually Costs You

Ransomware doesn't steal your files in the usual sense — it encrypts the records already sitting on your own drive, so you can no longer access your own data without paying for the key. That's a concrete example of how malware affects sensitive data: it doesn't have to leave your device to cause real damage.

18.2 Human Attacks

Cybersecurity has a reputation for being about clever code exploits. In reality, the majority of successful attacks target people, not systems — because it's almost always easier to trick someone than to break encryption.

18.2.1 Phishing & Its Tells

Phishing is an attack that tricks a person into surrendering credentials or data through a fake message pretending to be legitimate. The tells are consistent once you know to look for them:

  • A sender domain that's almost right but not quite.
  • Manufactured urgency — "your account will be deleted in 24 hours."
  • A link whose visible text doesn't match where it actually leads.
Breaking It Down: Phishing, Up Close

The name "phishing" comes from fishing: the attacker throws out bait and waits for someone to bite. The bait is a message that looks like it's from someone you trust, like your bank, a game company, a streaming service, or even your school, asking you to click a link, log in, or send information.

The first tell is a sender address that's almost right. Attackers use tricks like paypa1.com (a number 1 instead of an L) or a capital I instead of a lowercase l, which look nearly identical in many fonts. The second tell is urgency: "Act now or your account will be deleted!" Panic makes people skip checking.

The third tell is a mismatched link. The words of a link can say anything, like "netflix.com," while the real destination is somewhere else. On a computer, hover your mouse over a link without clicking, and the real address appears at the bottom of the screen. On a phone, press and hold the link to preview it.

When in doubt, don't click the link at all. Go to the real website or app yourself, the way you normally would, and check your account there. If there's really a problem, you'll see it. Real companies will never ask you to send your password by email or text.

Impersonation & Credential Stuffing

Impersonation: an account copying someone real to borrow trust you wouldn't extend to a stranger.

Credential stuffing: reusing a password leaked from one breach and trying that exact combination everywhere else — which is exactly why reusing passwords is so dangerous, even on accounts that feel unimportant.

Breaking It Down: Impersonation

Impersonation means pretending to be someone else, usually someone you trust, to get you to do something. Attackers copy a friend's profile picture and name to make a new account, or pretend to be a teacher, a boss, or a family member.

A common example: you get a message from what looks like your friend's account saying, "I'm locked out, can you send me the code that just got texted to you?" That code is really the login code for your account. If you send it, the attacker takes over your account, then uses it to trick your friends next.

Defense: if a request feels odd, especially one about money, passwords, or codes, check with the real person a different way, like calling them or asking them in person. A two-minute check can save your account.

Breaking It Down: Credential Stuffing

Websites get hacked all the time. Sometimes millions of usernames and passwords leak online. In credential stuffing, attackers take those leaked logins and try them on hundreds of other websites. A program does this automatically to see which ones work.

This works because many people reuse the same password everywhere. If you used the same password for an old game account and your email, a leak from that game hands attackers your email too. From your email, they can reset the passwords to many of your other accounts.

Defense: use a different password for every account, especially your email. Nobody can remember dozens of passwords, so use a password manager. Chapter 19 explains how they work. You can also check if your email has shown up in a known leak at the free site haveibeenpwned.com.

The trap: students often think cybersecurity is someone else's job — the IT department's. It's everyone's responsibility, because the weakest point in almost any system is a person who clicks the wrong link, not a flaw in the encryption itself.

18.3 Network Attacks & Real Defenses

18.1 and 18.2 covered malware and tricking people directly. Some attacks instead target the network connection itself, or brute-force their way past a login with no trickery at all.

18.3.1 Spear Phishing: Phishing, Aimed

Regular phishing (18.2) blasts the same fake message to thousands of people, hoping a few fall for it. Spear phishing is far more dangerous: one personalized message crafted specifically for a single target — like an attacker who researches a company's CFO and sends them a fake message that references real details, making it far more convincing than a generic scam.

Breaking It Down: Spear Phishing

Regular phishing is like casting a big net and hoping to catch a few fish. Spear phishing is like aiming a spear at one specific fish. The attacker picks one target and researches them first, using things like social media, company websites, and public information.

Then they write a message that uses real details: your boss's real name, a real project you're working on, the name of your school or team. Because it mentions things only an "insider" would know, it feels completely real. For example, an employee might get an email that looks like it's from their CEO asking them to quickly buy gift cards for a client.

Defense: be careful about what you share publicly, because attackers use it. Treat any unusual request, especially about money or passwords, as suspicious even if it mentions real details, and confirm it through a different channel before acting.

18.3.2 Attacking the Connection, Not the Person

  • Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) — an attacker secretly sits between two people's conversation, reading (or altering) messages neither person realizes are being intercepted.
  • Botnet — a network of hijacked, infected computers an attacker controls remotely, often without their real owners ever noticing.
  • Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) — a botnet floods a target's website with junk traffic all at once, so real customers can't get through.
Breaking It Down: Man-in-the-Middle Attacks

In a man-in-the-middle (MitM) attack, the attacker secretly places themselves between you and the website or person you're talking to. Everything you send passes through them first. They can read it, and sometimes change it, before passing it along, so neither side notices anything wrong.

It's like passing notes in class through another student who secretly reads each note, and sometimes rewrites it, before handing it on. A common setup is a fake Wi-Fi network named something like "Free_Airport_WiFi" that the attacker controls. Anyone who connects is sending their traffic straight through the attacker.

The main defense is encryption. When a website uses HTTPS (look for the padlock in the address bar), your data is scrambled so that even if someone intercepts it, they can't read it. Avoid logging into important accounts on public Wi-Fi, and never ignore browser warnings that say a connection isn't private.

Breaking It Down: Botnets

A botnet is an army of infected devices. Each one is called a bot or a "zombie." An attacker controls them all from far away. The owners usually have no idea. Their device seems normal, but in the background it quietly follows the attacker's orders.

Botnets don't only use computers. Any device on the internet can be taken over. That includes security cameras, smart TVs, and home routers, especially ones that still use the password they came with. In 2016, a botnet called Mirai took over hundreds of thousands of cameras and routers this way.

Attackers use botnets for big jobs that need lots of computers. They send spam, try millions of passwords, or launch DDoS attacks. Defense: change the starting password on every smart device, and keep them updated.

Breaking It Down: DDoS Attacks

A Denial of Service attack tries to make a website or service unusable by overwhelming it. A Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack does this from thousands of devices at once, usually a botnet, all sending junk traffic at the same target.

Picture a pizza shop that gets 10,000 fake phone orders in one minute. The phone lines are so jammed that real customers can't get through. A DDoS attack does the same thing to a website. The site isn't hacked or broken into. It's simply buried under more requests than it can answer.

Because the traffic comes from thousands of different places, it's hard to block just one source. Large websites defend themselves with special services that filter out junk traffic and spread the load across huge numbers of servers, the load-balancing idea from Chapter 17.

Why Public Wi-Fi Is an Easy Target

Traffic on an unsecured public Wi-Fi network is often unencrypted, which means an attacker on that same network can "sniff" (intercept) it in transit — the exact setup a Man-in-the-Middle attack needs. That coffee-shop Wi-Fi with no password is convenient specifically because nobody's checking who else is listening.

18.3.3 Brute Force & the Principle of Least Privilege

A brute force attack doesn't trick anyone — it's a program systematically trying password after password ("aaaa", "aaab", "aaac"...) until one finally works. It's slow, but it eventually succeeds against a short or simple password, which is exactly why length and complexity matter.

Breaking It Down: Brute Force & Password Strength

A brute force attack is like trying every key on a giant key ring until one opens the lock. A program guesses passwords one after another, very fast. Modern computers can try billions of guesses per second against stolen password data.

Math shows why length matters so much. A 4-digit PIN has only 10,000 possibilities, which a computer can try instantly. A password of 8 lowercase letters has about 209 billion possibilities. That sounds like a lot, but a fast computer can still crack it. Each extra character multiplies the number of guesses needed, so a long password is much harder to crack than a short one.

That's why a passphrase, several random words strung together like purple-tractor-ocean-banjo, is often stronger than a short, complicated password like Tr0b!. It's longer, so it's harder to brute-force, and it's easier to remember. Many websites also block brute force by locking an account after too many wrong guesses.

The Principle of Least Privilege limits the damage even a successful attack can do: give every account access only to what it actually needs for its job, nothing more. A part-time employee only given access to the specific systems their role requires — not the entire company's data — is following this principle.

Breaking It Down: The Principle of Least Privilege

The Principle of Least Privilege means each person, account, or program gets only the access it needs to do its job, and nothing more. It's like how a hotel key card opens only your room, not every room in the building.

Think about your school. Students can see their own grades, teachers can change grades for their own classes, and only a few administrators can change the whole grading system. If a student's account gets hacked, the attacker can't change anyone's grades, because that account never had that power.

The same idea protects your devices. When an app asks for permission to see your contacts, location, or microphone, ask yourself whether it really needs that to work. A flashlight app doesn't need your contacts. Saying no limits what a hacked or sketchy app could ever do.

Quick Check

Why does the Principle of Least Privilege limit damage even after an account is successfully hacked? (Answer: that account never had access to anything beyond what it actually needed, so a breach can't reach data or systems it was never given permission to touch.)

18.3.4 Firewalls & Zero-Days

A firewall monitors incoming and outgoing network traffic and blocks anything that doesn't match its security rules — a barrier between a trusted internal network and the open internet. But even a well-defended system can be caught off guard by a zero-day vulnerability: a flaw attackers are already actively exploiting before the software's own developer even knows it exists, meaning no official fix exists yet.

Breaking It Down: Firewalls

A firewall is like a security guard for network traffic. It checks every packet that comes in or goes out. It compares each one to a list of rules. Traffic that follows the rules gets through. Anything else is blocked.

Some rules are simple, like "only let outside traffic reach our website." Others are more detailed, like "don't let any computer here visit known harmful websites." Your home router has a basic firewall built in. Most computers have one too.

Firewalls are strong, but they can't stop everything. Say you download a Trojan or type your password into a phishing site. To a firewall, that looks like normal traffic. That's why security works in layers. Firewalls, updates, strong passwords, and careful users all work together.

Breaking It Down: Zero-Day Vulnerabilities

When a software maker learns about a security hole, they write a fix, called a patch, and send it out as an update. A zero-day vulnerability is a hole that attackers discover and start using before the software maker even knows it exists. The name means the developer has had "zero days" to fix it.

Zero-days are dangerous because there's no patch to install yet. Even people who update everything right away are exposed. Attackers can sell newly discovered zero-days for large amounts of money, because they work against everyone using that software.

Once a zero-day becomes public, companies rush to release a patch, sometimes within days. This is why installing updates promptly matters so much. The moment a fix exists, attackers start targeting everyone who hasn't installed it yet.
