Chapter 18: Cybersecurity Threats

What's the most common way an attacker actually gets into an account — a clever exploit, or something else entirely?

In this chapter: 18.1 Malware Families 18.2 Human Attacks 18.3 Network Attacks & Real Defenses

18.1 Malware Families

"Malware" isn't one thing — it's a category covering software with very different jobs, from quietly spying on you to actively holding your files hostage.

18.1.1 Five Families, Five Different Goals

TypeWhat It Actually Does
VirusAttaches itself to files and spreads when they're shared.
WormSpreads on its own across a network, no file-sharing required.
TrojanDisguises itself as legitimate software to get installed.
RansomwareEncrypts your files and extorts payment to unlock them.
SpywareCollects your data secretly, without your knowledge.
Worked Example: How Ransomware Actually Costs You

Ransomware doesn't steal your files in the usual sense — it encrypts the records already sitting on your own drive, so you can no longer access your own data without paying for the key. That's a concrete example of how malware affects sensitive data: it doesn't have to leave your device to cause real damage.

18.2 Human Attacks

Cybersecurity has a reputation for being about clever code exploits. In reality, the majority of successful attacks target people, not systems — because it's almost always easier to trick someone than to break encryption.

18.2.1 Phishing & Its Tells

Phishing is an attack that tricks a person into surrendering credentials or data through a fake message pretending to be legitimate. The tells are consistent once you know to look for them:

  • A sender domain that's almost right but not quite.
  • Manufactured urgency — "your account will be deleted in 24 hours."
  • A link whose visible text doesn't match where it actually leads.
Impersonation & Credential Stuffing

Impersonation: an account copying someone real to borrow trust you wouldn't extend to a stranger.

Credential stuffing: reusing a password leaked from one breach and trying that exact combination everywhere else — which is exactly why reusing passwords is so dangerous, even on accounts that feel unimportant.

The trap: students often think cybersecurity is someone else's job — the IT department's. It's everyone's responsibility, because the weakest point in almost any system is a person who clicks the wrong link, not a flaw in the encryption itself.

Quick Check

Why is cybersecurity described as everyone's responsibility, not just the IT department's? (Answer: most successful attacks target people through tricks like phishing, not the underlying systems themselves.)

18.3 Network Attacks & Real Defenses

18.1 and 18.2 covered malware and tricking people directly. Some attacks instead target the network connection itself, or brute-force their way past a login with no trickery at all.

18.3.1 Spear Phishing: Phishing, Aimed

Regular phishing (18.2) blasts the same fake message to thousands of people, hoping a few fall for it. Spear phishing is far more dangerous: one personalized message crafted specifically for a single target — like an attacker who researches a company's CFO and sends them a fake message that references real details, making it far more convincing than a generic scam.

18.3.2 Attacking the Connection, Not the Person

  • Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) — an attacker secretly sits between two people's conversation, reading (or altering) messages neither person realizes are being intercepted.
  • Botnet — a network of hijacked, infected computers an attacker controls remotely, often without their real owners ever noticing.
  • Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) — a botnet floods a target's website with junk traffic all at once, so real customers can't get through.
Why Public Wi-Fi Is an Easy Target

Traffic on an unsecured public Wi-Fi network is often unencrypted, which means an attacker on that same network can "sniff" (intercept) it in transit — the exact setup a Man-in-the-Middle attack needs. That coffee-shop Wi-Fi with no password is convenient specifically because nobody's checking who else is listening.

18.3.3 Brute Force & the Principle of Least Privilege

A brute force attack doesn't trick anyone — it's a program systematically trying password after password ("aaaa", "aaab", "aaac"...) until one finally works. It's slow, but it eventually succeeds against a short or simple password, which is exactly why length and complexity matter.

The Principle of Least Privilege limits the damage even a successful attack can do: give every account access only to what it actually needs for its job, nothing more. A part-time employee only given access to the specific systems their role requires — not the entire company's data — is following this principle.

Quick Check

Why does the Principle of Least Privilege limit damage even after an account is successfully hacked? (Answer: that account never had access to anything beyond what it actually needed, so a breach can't reach data or systems it was never given permission to touch.)

18.3.4 Firewalls & Zero-Days

A firewall monitors incoming and outgoing network traffic and blocks anything that doesn't match its security rules — a barrier between a trusted internal network and the open internet. But even a well-defended system can be caught off guard by a zero-day vulnerability: a flaw attackers are already actively exploiting before the software's own developer even knows it exists, meaning no official fix exists yet.
