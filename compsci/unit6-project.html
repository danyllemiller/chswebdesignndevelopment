Unit 6: Intermediate A&P — Project

The App Blueprint & Build Plan

Built in Google Sheets + Slides. 100 points.

Standards Addressed

Nevada Academic Content Standards for Computer Science & Integrated Technology: 9-12.AP.V.1 (linked lists/arrays vs. simple variables), 9-12.AP.M.1–2 (decompose into modules/procedures), 9-12.AP.PD.1 (design programs incorporating user feedback), 9-12.AP.PD.4 (team roles & collaborative tools), 9-12.AP.PD.5 (document design decisions) — the same standards Chapter 14 (The Array Refactor, The Structure Comparison), Chapter 15 (The Procedure Library, The Integrated Tool) and Chapter 16 (The Feedback Cycle, The License Audit, The Accessibility Pass) build toward.

Objective

Design a real app end-to-end — for a problem you or someone you actually know deals with, not a textbook example: model its data, break it into real modules, and document a full software-development-lifecycle pass on it — including real feedback you actually got and acted on. The real test of this project isn't whether it looks complete — it's whether you could explain to someone why this app should actually exist.

Deliverable: One Google Sheets tab (data structure comparison) + a Google Slides deck (everything else), minimum 6 slides. Turn in a PDF export of both, or a text file with your share links, below.

New to Google Slides or Sheets? Start here: Ch 4.0, The Office Suite, From Zero » · Spreadsheet Basics, From Zero »

Steps

Each step below links to the chapter section that actually teaches how to do it — do that first if you haven't already.

  1. Pick a real app idea that would solve an actual annoyance or gap in your life or someone else's — not "make school better," something specific: splitting gas money fairly on a carpool, tracking practice reps for a skill you're actually working on, finding what's already in the fridge before a grocery run, splitting chores evenly with siblings or roommates. It doesn't need to get built for real — you're designing it, not coding it — but it needs to be something you'd genuinely want to exist.
  2. Sheets: Data Structure Comparison — model a real chunk of your app's data both as a list/array-style table and as separate simple variables, then write out why the list/array version scales better as the data grows. How to: Ch 14.1, Arrays »
  3. Slides: Module Breakdown — decompose your app into at least 3 real modules/procedures (e.g. "input handling," "score calculation," "display"), each with a one-sentence job description. How to: Ch 15.1, Procedures, Parameters & Return Values »
  4. Slides: SDLC Walkthrough — document each phase: Planning, Design, a real Feedback round (get actual feedback from a classmate and show what you changed because of it), Testing, and one specific Accessibility finding + fix. How to: Ch 16.1, The Lifecycle & Real User Feedback » · Ch 16.3, Accessibility »
  5. Slides: License Check — list every outside resource, image, icon, or font you used and its license/attribution. How to: Ch 16.2, Licensing & Intellectual Property »
  6. Slides: Why This App, For Real — name one specific real person (it can be you) who would actually use this app, and the specific real moment where not having it causes a real problem. Not "it would be useful" — the actual moment.
Worked Example: What a Finished App Blueprint Looks Like

You may NOT use this app idea (a pet-feeding tracker) for your own project. It's here so you can see how every step connects to one app — pick your own real annoyance and follow the same pattern.

The App: “Who's Feeding the Dog?” — one tap logs that the dog was fed, so nobody in the family double-feeds or forgets.

Sheets: Data Structure Comparison

Separate simple variables

feeding1 = "Mon AM, Leo"
feeding2 = "Mon PM, Dad"
feeding3 = "Tue AM, Leo"
feeding4 = "Tue PM, ???"

One list/array table

DayMealFed ByTime
MonAMLeo7:05
MonPMDad6:30
TueAMLeo7:12

Why the list scales: After a year, the variable version needs 730 separately named variables, and checking “was the dog fed tonight?” means writing code that knows every name. The list just grows by one row per feeding, and one loop or one COUNTIF can search all of it.

Slides: Module Breakdown

ModuleJobInputs (parameters)Returns
logFeedingAdds a new row when someone taps “I fed him.”person, meal, timethe updated feeding list
wasFedChecks whether a meal has already been logged today.feedingList, day, mealtrue or false
buildReminderWrites the reminder text if a meal is overdue.meal, lastPersonWhoFeda message string, e.g. “Dinner not logged yet. Leo fed him last.”

Slides: SDLC Walkthrough

  • Planning: Users are the 4 people in one household. Goal: anyone can see in 2 seconds whether the dog ate.
  • Design: One screen with two big buttons (Breakfast, Dinner), each showing who fed him and when.
  • Feedback: Classmate Ari tested the sketch and said, “If two people tap at the same time, both think they fed him.” Change: after one tap, the button locks and shows “Fed by Leo, 7:12” instead of letting a second person log the same meal.
  • Testing: Tapped Breakfast twice from two phones. Expected: second tap shows “Already fed by Leo.” Result: passed after the lock was added.
  • Accessibility: Finding: “fed” and “not fed” were shown only as green vs. red, which some color-blind users can't tell apart. Fix: added a check-mark icon and the words “Fed” / “Not fed yet.”

Slides: Why This App, For Real

Leo Example, 15. Last Tuesday he got home at 6:00 and fed Biscuit; his dad got home at 6:45 and fed him again because nobody had said anything. Biscuit's vet had just put him on a diet, so the double dinner set the diet back. The app exists for that 45-minute gap.
