9.1 Decomposition

Ask a room to solve the attendance-tracking problem out loud and everyone jumps straight to a solution. Nobody asks what the actual parts are first — and a problem you can't break down is a problem you can't start.

9.1.1 The Method

Decomposition means breaking a large problem into smaller components through systematic analysis. The method is always the same four steps:

  1. State the problem in one sentence.
  2. List the required outcomes — what actually has to be true when you're done.
  3. Identify the distinct responsibilities inside it.
  4. Keep splitting each piece until it's small enough that one person could build and test it independently.
Worked Example: Decomposing Attendance

"Track attendance" splits into: recording who's present each block, storing that record somewhere, calculating a student's attendance rate, and flagging students below a threshold. Each of those is small enough to build and test on its own — and you'd build the recording piece first, since nothing else works without it.

Decomposition produces three kinds of containers for a single responsibility: procedures (a named block of reusable steps), modules (a group of related procedures), and objects (data and the procedures that act on it, bundled together). You'll build all three later in this course — for now, just know they exist as the destination decomposition is heading toward.

Quick Check

When is a subproblem small enough to stop splitting? (Answer: when one person could build and test it independently.)

Activity: The Decomposition Tree

Pick a real problem you actually notice at school or in your community — a slow lunch line, a confusing sign-up process for a club, a club's messy way of tracking attendance. Something specific, not "make school better."

  1. Write your problem at the top of a tree diagram (like the Attendance example above).
  2. Break it into smaller subproblems below it, then break at least one of those into even smaller pieces — three levels deep total, top to bottom.
  3. Keep splitting each branch until every box at the bottom is small enough that one person could actually build and test it alone.
  4. Circle the one piece you'd build first, and write one sentence explaining why nothing else can really start without it.

Turn In: Your three-level decomposition tree, with your first-piece choice circled and explained.

9.2 Algorithms & Pseudocode

Write instructions for making a sandwich precise enough that a machine could follow them — no assumed knowledge, no "you know what I mean." That's harder than it sounds, and it's exactly the discipline an algorithm demands.

9.2.1 What Makes Something an Algorithm

An algorithm is a sequence of instructions with three required properties: it's finite (it ends), unambiguous (only one possible interpretation), and it always terminates (it doesn't loop forever). Ambiguity is the enemy here, not length.

The Trap: "Obvious" Steps Aren't

Write "put peanut butter on the bread" and a literal executor might scoop the entire jar onto one square inch, or press the whole jar against the loaf. Every gap you leave, someone — or something — will fill in the worst possible way. That's why real algorithms spell out the boring parts too.

9.2.2 Writing Pseudocode

Pseudocode is how you draft an algorithm before touching real syntax — one instruction per line, explicit inputs and outputs, indentation showing structure, and no language-specific punctuation to trip over. A flowchart is the visual equivalent, and it's often clearer for anything with a lot of branching decisions.

Worked Example: Peer-Testing Pseudocode

Write pseudocode for a real task, then hand it to a partner and have them execute it literally — no filling in gaps, no assuming what you meant. Every place they get stuck is an ambiguity you missed. Revise until they can follow it start to finish without asking a single question.

Activity: The Pseudocode Spec

Take the one piece you circled at the bottom of your decomposition tree — the one you said you'd build first.

  1. Write pseudocode for it: one instruction per line, no assumed knowledge, spelling out even the "obvious" steps the way the sandwich example does.
  2. Draw a flowchart showing the same logic visually.
  3. Hand your pseudocode to a partner and have them execute it completely literally, out loud, filling in nothing you didn't actually write.
  4. Every place they get stuck or do something you didn't intend is a real ambiguity. Revise your pseudocode and repeat until they can follow it start to finish without asking you a single question.

Turn In: Your final pseudocode, your flowchart, and a note on at least one ambiguity your partner caught and how you fixed it.

9.3 Your First Prototype

Pseudocode proves your logic works on paper. A prototype is where you find out if it actually works — a working draft, not a finished product, built in Apps Script where a spreadsheet becomes programmable.

9.3.1 From Pseudocode to Real Code

Opening the script editor, writing a function, running it, granting permission, and reading the execution log — that loop is the entire workflow you'll repeat for the rest of this unit. Variables, assignment, and basic operators are simply the vocabulary your pseudocode gets translated into.

The trap: students often think an error message means they failed. It means the environment is doing its job — every error you read and fix is exactly how you learn what the code actually required, and no working script exists without a string of fixed errors behind it.

Quick Check

Why write pseudocode before writing real code? (Answer: it exposes ambiguity and logic errors before syntax gets in the way of finding them.)

Activity: The First Prototype

Turn your revised, partner-tested pseudocode into real, running code.

  1. Open the Apps Script editor and translate your pseudocode line by line into a real function.
  2. Run it. You will almost certainly get an error the first time — that's expected, not a failure.
  3. Fix the error, run it again, and repeat until it runs successfully and produces real output in the execution log.
  4. For every error you hit along the way, write one sentence on what caused it and what fixing it taught you.

Turn In: Your working code, a screenshot of its successful output, and your error log with what each one taught you.
