The Most Common Mistake

What's the #1 mistake new developers make? They build a website for themselves.

They use the colors they like. They add the features they think are cool. They organize the navigation based on what they think is logical. But unless you are the only person who will ever visit your site, this is a recipe for disaster.

Big secret: You are not your user.

You might be a 15-year-old gamer who loves high-contrast neon. Your user might be a 65-year-old grandma looking for a knitting pattern. She doesn't want flashing animations; she wants big, readable text and a clear "Print Recipe" button.

If you don't know who you're building for, you will fail. UX Design is about empathy—the ability to see the world through someone else's eyes and build something that solves their specific needs.

Phase 1: The Research (How to Find "The User")

You can't build a useful profile based on a guess. You need hard data. In professional web design, we rely on two distinct types of research to build our plan for a site like PixelPlay (our retro-gaming community).

Research Type Qualitative (The "Why") Quantitative (The "What" & "How Many")
Goal To understand why users behave a certain way. Explores feelings and pain points. To measure what users do. Gathers statistical data to validate hypotheses.
Sample Size Small (5-8 people is often enough to find patterns) Large (100+ responses to be statistically useful)
Methods User Interviews, Field Studies, Usability Tests Surveys, A/B Tests, Web Analytics
Question Type Open-ended: "Walk me through your day..." Closed-ended: "How many times a week do you..."
Data Output Quotes, observations, insights, and stories. Charts, graphs, percentages, and statistics.

Digging for the "Why" and Proving the "What"

User Interviews: Conversations with people representing your target audience. We look for Pain Points. If a user says, "I hate digging through a 50-page forum just to find one answer," they've told you exactly what to fix.

Pro Tip: Don't "Lead the Witness." Instead of "Don't you hate GameFAQs?", ask "Walk me through the last time you used it. What was that like?"

Surveys: Numbers to back up your stories. If 80% of 200 responses say they use their Phone while playing on a Desktop/TV, your site must be mobile-first.

Phase 3: The Competition (SWOT & Matrix)

To beat the competition, you first have to understand them. We use SWOT Analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) to find where they are vulnerable.

SWOT Analysis: The 2×2 Grid

Strengths (Internal)

What does your site/brand do better than the competition? These are advantages you control — unique features, strong content, brand loyalty.

Weaknesses (Internal)

Where are you falling short? What do competitors do better? These are gaps you control and can work to fix — slow load times, thin content, weak design.

Opportunities (External)

What is happening in the market that you can take advantage of? Trends, underserved audiences, topics no one covers well — your "Blue Ocean."

Threats (External)

What outside forces could hurt you? New competitors, algorithm changes, shifting user behavior, emerging technologies that could make your approach obsolete.

Internal factors (you control) are on the left column. External factors (market controls) are on the right column.

Written Guides (Deep)❓✅❌❌
Video Guides❌❌❌✅
High Score Leaderboards❓❌❌❌

Phase 4: The Features (User Stories & MoSCoW)

The Why: A feature list like "add a leaderboard" tells a developer what to build but not why — so they make a dozen small decisions guessing at intent. A feature list built from real benefits keeps every decision traceable back to an actual user need.

The What: We write User Stories to keep focus on benefits, not just tasks: "As a [type of user], I want to [do a thing] so that I can [achieve a goal]." The final clause — the "so that" — is the part most people skip, and it's the part that actually matters.

"As a community member, I want to submit my high score with a screenshot so that I can get on the leaderboard."
Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often write "As a user, I want a leaderboard" and stop — that's a feature request wearing a User Story costume. Without the "so that" goal, nobody can tell whether a leaderboard that only shows the top 3 scores actually satisfies the real need, or whether the user really wanted to compare against their friends specifically.
The MoSCoW Method (MVP Priority)

The How: Once you have real User Stories, MoSCoW forces hard priority decisions instead of an endless wishlist:

PriorityStands ForDefinition
MMust HaveThe site is useless without this. (The "M" in MVP).
SShould HaveVery important, but not a deal-breaker.
CCould HaveA "nice to have" bonus.
WWon't HaveOut of scope for "Release 1."

If every feature ends up "Must Have," the method has failed — the whole point is forcing real trade-offs before a single line of code gets written.

Phase 5: The Workflow (The "ADDIE" Model)

The Why: Right now you have research, a persona, a competitive matrix, and a feature list — a pile of separate documents. Without a shared process, three teammates building the same site will each decide, on their own, when "planning" ends and "building" begins. That's how projects miss deadlines and ship things nobody asked for.

The What: The ADDIE Model is a 5-step cycle — Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate — used to build everything from websites to video games to this very course. It gives every person on a team the same shared map of "where are we right now."

  • A - Analyze: User research, Persona, User Stories (Phase 1-4).
  • D - Design: Creating the Sitemap and Wireframes (Phase 5).
  • D - Develop: Actually writing the code (HTML, CSS, JS).
  • I - Implement: Launching the site on a web host.
  • E - Evaluate: Post-launch data analysis and user feedback.

The How: This chapter's Milestone project is you actually walking the first two steps: the Persona and research you built are the Analyze phase on paper, and the wireframe you'll design is the Design phase — a real, demonstrated pass through the cycle, not just a memorized diagram.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often picture ADDIE as a straight line you walk once. In practice it's a loop — the Evaluate step routinely sends you straight back to Analyze when user feedback reveals the original research missed something. A site is never "done"; it cycles.
Quick Check:

The Sitemap (The "Map")

The Why: Right now, your MoSCoW list is just a pile of features with no home. Without a plan for where each one lives, two developers on the same team will build conflicting navigation — one puts "Leaderboards" under a "Community" menu, another puts it under "Games," and users can't find either.

The What: A Sitemap is a simple, top-down diagram that shows every page on a website and how those pages connect — an appropriate navigation system planned before any code exists. It starts with the Homepage (index.html) at the top, branching down into parent categories and their child pages.

The How: This turns your abstract "User Stories" into a concrete list of HTML files you need to build. If Ryan's story is "I want to find a guide," your sitemap needs a /guides/ parent page — not a vague promise that guides exist "somewhere."

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often build a sitemap that's just a flat list of every page with no grouping — the digital equivalent of a junk drawer. A real navigation system groups related pages under a small number of clear parent categories; if you can't summarize your whole site in 5-7 top-level nav items, the sitemap needs more structure, not more pages.

The Wireframe (The "Blueprint")

A Wireframe is a black-and-white blueprint for a single page. It has NO colors, NO fonts, and NO real images. It only focuses on layout, hierarchy, and priority.

Levels of "Fidelity" (Detail)
  • Low-Fidelity (Lo-Fi): Fast paper sketches for rapid ideation.
  • Mid-Fidelity (Mid-Fi): Clean, digital black-and-white version in Figma.
  • High-Fidelity (Hi-Fi): Pixel-perfect mockup ready for coding.
Why no color? Because if you use color, you'll waste 20 minutes talking about blue instead of what really matters: "Is the button in the right place?"
Wireframing for Responsive Design

Ryan checks PixelPlay on his phone between matches and on his desktop when he's writing a full walkthrough. A single wireframe isn't enough — you plan at least three Breakpoints (screen-width ranges where the layout changes): Mobile (~375px), Tablet (~768px), and Desktop (~1280px+). The content doesn't change between them. The layout does — a 3-column game grid on desktop might stack into a single scrolling column on mobile.

A common trap: sketching only the desktop version and assuming "it'll just shrink." It won't — a 3-column layout squeezed onto a phone screen becomes unreadable. Wireframe the mobile version first (this is called Mobile-First Design), since it forces you to prioritize what truly matters when space is tightest.

Quick Check:

Why is it risky to wireframe only the desktop version of a page and assume mobile will "just work"?

Check your answer

A layout doesn't automatically resize in a usable way — multi-column layouts can become cramped or unreadable on a phone. Wireframing mobile first forces you to prioritize what fits in the smallest space.

Planning UI with "Atomic Design"

The Why: Without a shared vocabulary, "redesign the search bar" means something different to every developer on the team — one rebuilds the whole thing from scratch, another just recolors it. Atomic Design gives every piece of a UI a name, the same way chemistry names atoms and molecules, so the whole team can talk about the exact same piece.

The What: We build interfaces like LEGOs — the smallest parts combine into bigger, reusable parts:

Atoms
The smallest, indivisible HTML elements: <button>, <input>, <label>. An atom on its own usually isn't useful yet.
Molecules
A small group of atoms working together as one functional unit — like a Search Form (Label + Input + Button).
Organisms
Several molecules and atoms combined into a complex, standalone section — like a Site Header or Game Grid.

The How: Here's PixelPlay's search feature built up in real HTML, one level at a time:

Step 1 — Atoms (individually meaningless)
<label>Search Guides</label>
<input type="text" placeholder="Earthbound...">
<button>Go</button>
Step 2 — Molecule (the atoms now do a job together)
<form class="search-form">
  <label>Search Guides</label>
  <input type="text" placeholder="Earthbound...">
  <button>Go</button>
</form>
Step 3 — Organism (the molecule joins other molecules)
<header class="site-header">
  <img class="logo" src="pixelplay-logo.png" alt="PixelPlay logo">
  <nav>...</nav>
  <form class="search-form">...</form> <!-- our molecule, reused here -->
</header>
Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think Atomic Design means literally building a page atom-by-atom in some rigid order. It's a naming and reuse system, not a build sequence — the real payoff is that the search-form molecule gets written once and reused in the header, the sidebar, and the footer without being rebuilt from scratch each time.

Understanding "How" Users Read

The Why: You can have a perfect wireframe with the right feature in the right priority order, and users will still miss it — if it isn't placed where their eyes actually travel. Eye-tracking studies on real websites found people don't read pages top-to-bottom like a book; they scan in one of two predictable shapes depending on what kind of page it is.

The What: The F-Pattern and Z-Pattern are the two dominant scanning shapes, and each fits a different kind of content.

F-Pattern

Used for text-heavy pages (blogs, forums, long guides). Users read a full line across the top, then scan down the left edge reading only the first few words of each line, occasionally sweeping right on a line that grabs their attention — tracing a shape that looks like the letter F.

Z-Pattern

Used for simple, visual pages (homepages, landing pages) with far less text. Users scan top-left to top-right (the logo, then the nav), diagonally down across the middle of the page, then along the bottom-left to bottom-right — tracing a Z.

The How: This directly changes where you put things on PixelPlay's pages. On the text-heavy walkthrough page (F-Pattern), the most important information — "which boss, which strategy" — has to be in the first few words of the first line of each paragraph, because that's the only part of most lines a scanning reader actually processes. On the simple homepage (Z-Pattern), the logo goes top-left, the primary call-to-action button goes bottom-right — exactly where the Z-scan naturally ends, so it's the last thing a user's eye lands on before they decide what to click.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often assume the pattern is fixed by the type of website ("blogs use F, homepages use Z") rather than by the actual content on that specific page. A homepage stuffed with three paragraphs of text will get scanned in an F-pattern regardless of what type of page it "is" — the amount and density of text on the page determines the pattern, not the page's category.

Writing for the Web: Voice, Tone & Purpose

The Why: A wireframe with perfect layout still fails if the words on it are wrong for the reader. If PixelPlay's homepage sounds like a corporate press release, Ryan bounces in five seconds — he came for a fellow gamer's site, not a annual report.

The What: Voice is your brand's personality — it stays consistent everywhere (PixelPlay's voice: knowledgeable, a little nerdy, never condescending). Tone is how that voice adjusts to a specific moment — the tone on a "404 Page Not Found" page is playful and reassuring, while the tone on a "Payment Failed" error is calm and clear, even though the voice underneath is the same. Style is the concrete writing rules that make voice consistent: sentence length, contractions allowed or not, how numbers are written, whether headlines use title case.

The How: Compare these two homepage headlines for PixelPlay, both technically correct:

Wrong Voice for This Audience

Matches Ryan's Voice
"Stuck on a boss fight? Get the guide, drop your high score, and find people who actually remember this game."

Both sentences describe the same site. Only one sounds like it was written by someone who plays these games.

Every Page Has a Job: Primary vs. Secondary Purpose

Before writing a word, name the page's Primary Purpose — the one thing it must accomplish — and its Secondary Purpose — a smaller, supporting goal it can also achieve without competing for attention. A game guide page's primary purpose is helping Ryan beat the boss. Its secondary purpose might be getting him to create a free account so he can save his progress. If the account signup banner is bigger than the actual walkthrough text, the page has confused its priorities and will frustrate the exact user it was built for.

Who's Actually Reading This? Audience & Reading Level

You already met your target audience back in Phase 2 — that's what a Persona is for. But audience isn't just demographics; it's also reading level: how complex your sentences and vocabulary can be before you lose the reader. A children's museum site should write at roughly a 3rd–5th grade reading level. A legal-services site can write at a college level, because that audience expects and trusts more formal language. Tools like the Hemingway App or Microsoft Word's built-in Flesch-Kincaid checker score a reading level automatically — professionals check this before publishing, they don't guess.

For Ryan and PixelPlay, the target is roughly an 8th-grade reading level: casual enough to feel like a friend explaining a strategy, clear enough that a walkthrough doesn't require re-reading a sentence three times.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think "professional" automatically means "formal and complex." For most commercial sites, the opposite is true — a high reading level and stiff corporate voice actively drive casual users away. Match the reading level and voice to the audience you researched, not to what sounds impressive to write.
Quick Check:

A walkthrough page's account-signup banner is bigger and bolder than the actual walkthrough. What content mistake is this?

Check your answer

The secondary purpose (signup) is competing with — and overshadowing — the primary purpose (helping the user beat the boss). The primary purpose should always be the most visually dominant thing on the page.

Say It Right: Grammar & Proofreading

The Why: A typo on a business card is embarrassing. A typo on a website is permanent, public, and indexed by Google forever — and it quietly tells every visitor "this business doesn't check its work." For an agency selling trust to clients like Marisol or PixelPlay, sloppy copy costs credibility before a client even reads the pitch.

The What: Applying grammar and spelling conventions means proofreading content against the same standard every time: correct spelling, subject-verb agreement, consistent capitalization (is it "e-mail" or "email"? Pick one and stay consistent site-wide), and punctuation that matches your chosen style.

The How: Professionals build a simple Style Sheet — a one-page list of the specific spelling and formatting choices for a project — so five different writers all write "sign up" (two words, verb) the same way instead of mixing in "signup" and "sign-up" across different pages. Run a spell-checker, but don't trust it blindly: it won't catch "their" vs. "there," or a game title spelled two different ways on two different pages.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think a clean spell-check pass means the content is done. Spell-checkers miss correctly-spelled wrong words ("Their are three modes") and inconsistency across pages (a game titled "Earthbound" on one page and "Earth Bound" on another). Read content out loud — your ear catches errors your eyes skim past.

Try it yourself: Proofread this real excerpt a student wrote for a client site, and list every issue you find: "Are agency provide affordable Web sites for local bussiness's. we make sure you're site looks great on Mobile and Desktop." (Answer: "Are" should be "Our"; "Web sites" should be consistently "websites"; "bussiness's" is a misspelled plural, not possessive — should be "businesses"; the sentence starting "we" needs a capital "We"; "Mobile and Desktop" shouldn't be capitalized mid-sentence.) This is exactly the kind of pass you'll run on every page before it goes live for a real client.

Helping Google Find You (SEO)

SEO (Search Engine Optimization) is the science of ranking #1. Focus on the "Holy Trinity":

The <title> Tag Tells Google the main topic.

Good: <title>Super Metroid Walkthrough | PixelPlay</title>
<meta description> Your "ad" snippet.

Good: Need a Super Metroid guide? See our maps and secrets...
The <h1> Tag The visible headline. You should only have ONE per page so search engines know exactly what the page is about.
The Holy Trinity — What It Looks Like in Real HTML
<!-- 1. The <title> tag — appears in the browser tab and as the Google link -->
<title>Super Metroid Walkthrough & Boss Guide | PixelPlay</title>

<!-- 2. The <meta description> — the "ad copy" shown under your link in Google results -->
<meta name="description"
      content="Complete Super Metroid walkthrough with maps, boss strategies, and sequence breaks. Updated 2024.">

<!-- 3. The <h1> — the main visible headline; one per page only -->
<h1>Super Metroid Complete Walkthrough</h1>

All three should include your target keyword. Google reads them as a confirmation that this page is genuinely about that topic.

Short-Tail vs. Long-Tail Keywords

Short-Tail Keyword

"RPG games"

  • Extremely high search volume
  • Extremely high competition — you're competing with Wikipedia, IGN, Metacritic
  • Vague intent — user might want a list, a review, a forum, or a store
  • As a new site, you will not rank here
Long-Tail Keyword

"best SNES RPGs with time travel"

  • Lower search volume — but the people who search it are exactly your audience
  • Very low competition — fewer pages target this specific phrase
  • High intent — user knows exactly what they want
  • This is where new sites win their first rankings

Strategy: Build 10 pages targeting long-tail keywords and own those niches before competing for the big terms.

Beyond Keywords: Content Habits That Actually Rank

The Holy Trinity gets a page found once. These habits are what keep Google — and your readers — coming back:

  • One H1, a real heading hierarchy below it. Use <h2> and <h3> to break content into scannable sections — Google uses this structure to understand what each part of the page is about.
  • Descriptive alt text on every image. Google can't "see" a screenshot, but it reads the alt text. "Boss fight screenshot" beats "img047.png" for both SEO and accessibility.
  • Internal links between related pages. Link the Earthbound walkthrough to the Earthbound leaderboard. This keeps visitors on the site longer and tells Google the pages are related.
  • Fresh, regularly updated content. A guide updated after a game patch ranks better than an identical one abandoned for three years.
  • Fast page load and mobile-friendly layout. Google actively penalizes slow, non-responsive pages in rankings — this is exactly why the Wireframing for Responsive Design section above matters for SEO, not just usability.

Try it yourself: For a PixelPlay walkthrough of a game you know, write down 1 short-tail keyword and 3 long-tail keyword phrases a real player might actually search. That's the exact keyword list a professional writes before drafting a single sentence of content.

Getting Found the Other Way: Social Media & the Web

The Why: SEO gets PixelPlay found when someone already knows what they're searching for. But Ryan didn't find his favorite gaming Discord server by Googling it — a friend shared a link in a Reddit thread. Search and social are two different doors into the same site, and a professional agency has to plan for both.

The What: Social media's role in web development isn't just "post about your site sometimes." It shapes real technical and content decisions: what images look like when shared, what gets written to be shareable, and where traffic actually comes from before it ever hits a search engine.

How Social Platforms and Web Links Actually Connect

Every time a link is shared on Discord, X, or Facebook, that platform reads special Open Graph meta tags in your site's <head> to build the preview card — the image, title, and description shown in the post. A site without Open Graph tags shares as a bare, ugly link with no image, and gets far fewer clicks. This is a direct, technical correlation between a social platform and your web page's own code:

<meta property="og:title" content="Super Metroid Walkthrough | PixelPlay">
<meta property="og:description" content="Maps, boss strategies, and sequence breaks.">
<meta property="og:image" content="/images/metroid-share-card.png">

Without these three lines, every share of the walkthrough looks broken. With them, it looks professional — and professional-looking shares get more clicks, which sends more visitors back to the site.

Advertising, Marketing, Social Media & the Website: One Ecosystem

These four pieces work as a loop, not separate jobs: Marketing sets the goal (get 500 new visitors this month). Social media is one channel used to reach that goal (post 3x/week). Advertising can boost specific posts to reach people who don't already follow the account. The website is where all of that traffic has to land somewhere useful — a beautiful ad campaign that dumps visitors on a confusing homepage wastes the entire budget. As an agency, you don't just build the site; you're often the one explaining this whole loop to a client who only asked for "a Facebook page."

Current Trends Worth Knowing
  • Short-form vertical video (TikTok, Reels, Shorts) now drives more discovery traffic for many small businesses than search does.
  • Algorithm-driven feeds mean posting isn't enough — platforms decide who sees it, and engagement in the first hour heavily influences reach.
  • Community platforms like Discord function as a site's "back channel" — direct, real-time connection with the exact users a business most needs to retain (this is exactly how Ryan stays connected to PixelPlay's community).
  • The Creator Economy (introduced back in Chapter 1) means a single trusted streamer or reviewer linking to a site can outperform a paid ad campaign.
Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often treat "make a social strategy" as "post whenever we remember to." A real strategy names a specific platform, a specific audience action (click the link, join the Discord, share the post), and a specific link destination on the site — not just visibility for its own sake.

Try it yourself: Draft a 3-post launch plan for PixelPlay across one week. For each post, name the platform, one sentence of the actual post content, and exactly which page on the site it links to. That's a real, if small, social media strategy — the same document a client would expect from your agency before a launch.

🏆 Milestone: Project 1 - The Blueprint [100 pts]

Objective: Prove technical and architectural competence by designing a Mid-Fidelity wireframe blueprint for an agency project. This Milestone is where the Analyze and Design phases of the ADDIE model become a real, demonstrated artifact — not just a diagram you memorized.

  1. Based on your User Persona, MoSCoW MVP features, and Sitemap from this week, design the actual layout of your Homepage and one sub-page.
  2. THE RULE: You must create a Mid-Fidelity (Mid-Fi) Wireframe. This means strictly Black, White, and Grey. No colors, no real images, no fancy fonts. Use boxes with an "X" through them to represent where images will eventually go.
  3. Go Responsive: Draw both a Desktop version AND a Mobile version of your Homepage. Show how your layout's hierarchy adapts — what stacks, what shrinks, what disappears into a menu.
  4. Focus entirely on Visual Hierarchy: Make sure your most important MVP feature is the biggest, most prominent thing on the screen.
  5. Peer Review: Share your wireframe with an agency partner. Have them point out the first 3 things their eyes go to. If it's not what you intended, adjust your layout!

Turn In: Upload your Mid-Fi Wireframe image files (Desktop + Mobile versions).
