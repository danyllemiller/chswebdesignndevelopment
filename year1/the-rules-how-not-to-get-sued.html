The moment you publish a website, you become a publisher. You take on the same kind of legal responsibility as a newspaper, a magazine, or a TV station, whether you meant to or not.

This chapter is where you stop being someone who "makes stuff work" and start being someone who makes it legal, secure, and fair — the difference between a hobbyist and a professional the Carson High Web Design & Marketing Agency can actually hire. Skip this chapter and you're one careless image download away from costing a real client real money. Learn it, and you become the person clients trust with their business.

Every rule below is really just an answer to one of Marisol's questions.

Whose Stuff Is This Anyway? A Guide to Intellectual Property

The Why: Remember the $2,500 photo from the opening story? That happens because most people assume "I found it online" means "I can use it." It doesn't. If you build Marisol's site with a stolen photo, the lawsuit doesn't land on you — it lands on her, because she owns the site. Understanding ownership isn't a legal technicality; it's the difference between protecting your client and quietly ruining her business.

The What: Everything someone creates with their mind is their Intellectual Property (IP) — property that exists as an idea or creative work rather than a physical object. IP law gives creators the exclusive right to control how their work is used. There are three main "force fields" that protect different kinds of creations, and knowing which one applies changes what you're allowed to do.

1. Copyright © (The Automatic Shield)

Copyright is automatic legal protection for creative work. The second you write a poem, snap a photo, record a video, or code a cool JavaScript game, it's copyrighted. You own it. You don't have to file any paperwork, register with a fancy office, or even type the © symbol. This shield protects books, music, photos, videos, articles, and yes, even software code. You can research the law further at the U.S. Copyright Office.

Work for Hire: If you are a professional developer, your contract will likely state that the company owns the copyright to anything you build on the clock. This is called Work Made for Hire — the person who paid for the work owns it, not the person who made it.

2. Trademark ™ (The Brand Sentry)

A trademark doesn't protect a creative work; it protects a brand — a specific word, phrase, logo, or even a sound that represents a company. Think of the Nike "swoosh," the name "Coca-Cola," or the golden arches of McDonald's. You can't just slap a famous logo on your website to sell t-shirts. That's called infringement, and it's a great way to get a very scary, very real letter from a team of expensive lawyers. Check the USPTO database to see what brands are protected.

3. Patent ℗ (The Invention Ledger)

A patent protects a new, specific, and non-obvious way of doing something — a new type of smartphone technology, a medical device, or a piece of software like Amazon's original 1-Click purchase system. Unlike copyright and trademark, a patent is not automatic; you must apply for one, and it expires after about 20 years, at which point it enters the Public Domain.

The How: Say Marisol wants her truck's logo, a custom illustrated tamale mascot, on the homepage. Here's how you'd reason through it: the illustration itself is protected the instant the artist draws it (copyright). If Marisol wants to stop competitors from using a similar logo to confuse customers, she needs to register it with the USPTO (trademark). If she invented some genuinely new food-truck ordering process — say, a novel way to route mobile orders — that would be a patent question, not a copyright one. Three different questions, three different answers, and mixing them up is the single most common mistake in this unit.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think "copyright" is the umbrella term for all of IP law. It isn't — it's one of three separate protections. A related trap: students assume a patent, like copyright, is automatic. It is not. If you don't file the paperwork, there's no patent, no matter how original the invention.

The Sharing Economy: Creative Commons and the Public Domain

The Why: Does the "Don't steal stuff" rule mean you have to create every single pixel on Marisol's site from scratch? No — and that would be a terrible business model. Many creators want to share their work. You just have to know the rules they attached to it, or you're back to square one: liable for using something you didn't have permission for.

The What: Creative Commons (CC) is a set of standard licenses creators attach to their work so others can legally reuse it. Think of it as a "How You Can Share My Food" menu. Instead of the default "All Rights Reserved" of copyright, CC provides a range of "Some Rights Reserved" options, each with its own rules:

  • Attribution (BY): "You can have my pizza, but you must tell everyone I made it." This is the core requirement of almost all CC licenses.
  • NonCommercial (NC): "You can have my pizza, but you can't sell slices of it." You cannot make money using these assets. (Your agency cannot use NC assets for paying clients like Marisol!)
  • NoDerivatives (ND): "You can have my pizza, but you can't add pineapples." You cannot edit, crop, or filter the work.
  • ShareAlike (SA): If you remix the work, you must share your final product under the exact same license.

The How: These licenses stack. "BY-NC" means "Give me credit and don't use it to make money." If Marisol's site is a commercial project (it is — she's selling tamales), any image licensed BY-NC is off-limits, full stop, no matter how perfect it looks. Before you use any CC-licensed work, read the license at CreativeCommons.org and follow it exactly.

Public Domain & The "Fair Use" Trap

The Public Domain is like a "free buffet." When a copyright expires or a creator voluntarily gives up all rights, their work belongs to everyone. You can use it for anything, no credit needed. Check Project Gutenberg for examples.

What about Fair Use? Fair Use is a limited legal defense, not a right. It weighs four factors: purpose (educational vs. commercial), nature of the work, amount used, and effect on the market. In the professional guild, we avoid the Fair Use gray area entirely and stick to licensed or original content — Marisol's business use makes the "commercial" factor work against you every time.

Citing Third-Party Work: Where the Line Actually Is

The Why: "I gave credit" is not the same as "I was allowed to use it." Students constantly confuse the two, and it's the single fastest way to accidentally break a license.

The What: A proper citation for third-party web assets records three things: the creator's name, the exact license (with a link to it), and the source URL. This isn't optional busywork — it's your legal paper trail proving you had permission.

The How: Here's a real citation, the kind you'll write in Lab 1, for a photo licensed CC BY 4.0:

Photo: "Downtown Carson City Storefront"
Creator: J. Alvarez, via Flickr
License: CC BY 4.0 — https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/
Source: https://flickr.com/photos/example/123456
Used with attribution as required by license.

That's the boundary: you may crop, resize, and place that photo anywhere on Marisol's site, including her paid ordering page, as long as this citation exists somewhere reasonable (a credits page or a footer note) and you never claim you took the photo yourself.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think crediting a photo automatically makes it legal to use — "I put the photographer's name in the footer, so I'm covered." Attribution satisfies the BY condition of a CC license; it does not override NC or ND restrictions, and it does nothing at all for a fully copyrighted image with no license attached. Credit is one requirement among several, not a magic word.

Build a Fortress: Web Security and User Privacy

The Why: A great website isn't just legal — it's safe. When Marisol's customers type their email into her site to join a newsletter, they're trusting her (and you) with their data. Building a website without thinking about privacy is like building a house without locks: fine, until it isn't.

Your Digital Ghost: Footprints & Privacy

The What: Every single thing you do online — your searches, your "likes," your comments, every site you visit — is tracked and recorded. This massive trail of data is your Digital Footprint. Think of it like an internet tattoo: permanent, searchable, and something people will judge you for. Explore your own footprint with tools like the EFF's Privacy Badger.

The How: Now imagine Marisol's site collects customer emails for a "10% off your next order" newsletter, and six months later, she sells that email list to a marketing company for extra cash — without telling anyone. That's Invasion of Privacy: collecting or sharing PII (Personally Identifiable Information) without the customer's permission. As the developer who built the signup form without a privacy policy or consent checkbox, you made that possible. That's not just unethical; under several of the laws in this chapter, it's illegal.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think "invasion of privacy" only means hacking or stealing data. It doesn't require a hacker at all — simply collecting more data than you disclosed, or sharing it with a third party the user never agreed to, is enough to count.

The Secret Handshake: HTTP vs. HTTPS

The Why: Have you ever noticed some website addresses start with http:// and others with https://? That one little "s" is arguably the most important letter in all of web security, and it's the difference between Marisol's customer typing their address into a form safely, or a stranger on the same coffee-shop Wi-Fi reading it in plain text.

The What: HTTP (Hypertext Transfer Protocol) is an unsecure protocol — like sending a postcard. Anyone who intercepts it can read everything on it. HTTPS (Secure) is a secure protocol — like sending a sealed, armored letter. The information is encrypted (scrambled into an unbreakable secret code). You get the "s" and the padlock icon 🔒 by installing a Security Certificate (SSL/TLS), which a trusted Certificate Authority (CA) issues after verifying the site is really who it claims to be.

The SSL/TLS Handshake (The 8-Step Secret)

The How: This is exactly what happens, in order, every time a browser loads an HTTPS site:

  1. Request: Browser asks for a secure session.
  2. Certificate: Server sends its SSL Certificate and Public Key.
  3. Verification: Browser checks the certificate with the CA.
  4. Session Key Creation: Browser creates a unique, temporary Session Key.
  5. Asymmetric Encryption: Browser encrypts that Session Key using the server's Public Key.
  6. Private Unlock: Server uses its Private Key to unlock the message.
  7. Mutual Knowledge: Now both share a secret "code" that no one else knows.
  8. Symmetric Encryption: All data is now scrambled using that secret key.

That certificate is what proves the site loading in the browser is really Marisol's site, and not an impostor page set up to steal customer card numbers. That's exactly how a security certificate protects a website: it verifies identity and encrypts the connection at the same time.

The Security Checklist: Practices Every Site Should Model

Knowing what HTTPS is doesn't protect anyone unless you actually build it in — the same way knowing what a smoke detector is doesn't stop a fire unless you install one. "Modeling acceptable security practices" means you don't just know the rules; you demonstrably follow them on every client site, by default, without being asked. Here's the checklist you'll apply to Marisol's project:

  • ALWAYS use HTTPS. Period. Test it with the Qualys SSL Test.
  • Clear Privacy Policy. A page in plain English telling customers exactly what data you're collecting and why.
  • Data Protection. Never store passwords in plain text; use hashing and salting — a one-way scrambling process — so that even if a database is stolen, the passwords inside it are useless to the thief.
  • Data Minimization. If Marisol only needs an email for the newsletter, don't also collect a phone number "just in case."

Try it yourself: write a one-paragraph "Security & Privacy Practices" statement for Vega's Tamale Co., in language Marisol herself could read, covering HTTPS, password handling, and what customer data you will and won't collect. That's the artifact a professional agency hands a client before launch — and it's how you demonstrate, not just describe, acceptable security practice.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think HTTPS only matters if a site has a login form or takes payments, and that "security" is entirely the server's job. Any site — even a "read-only" blog — leaks browsing behavior without HTTPS, and plenty of security failures (plaintext passwords, forms with no encryption, collecting data you don't need) are decisions made directly in the code and content you write.

Privacy Laws: A Global Landscape

The Why: If a law only protected people in the state where a business is based, the internet would make it meaningless — anyone anywhere can visit Marisol's site. So governments write privacy laws that apply based on where the visitor lives, not where the business is. That means you, as the developer, have to think globally even for a local food truck.

The What: First is the GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) from Europe. It uses an "OPT-IN" model — you cannot collect data unless the user gives specific consent. It also grants the "Right to be Forgotten," where users can demand their data be deleted. Failure to comply can cost a company 4% of global revenue.

Here in Nevada, we have the Nevada Consumer Privacy Act (SB220). It uses an "OPT-OUT" model focused on the Sale of Data. It gives residents the right to tell a website, "STOP SELLING MY DATA" via a clear link. That's the American philosophy of "Business First, with Consumer Protection," versus Europe's "Privacy First."

The How: For Marisol's newsletter signup, GDPR means the checkbox must start unchecked — a customer in the EU has to actively click it. SB220 means Nevada residents just need a visible link that says something like "Do Not Sell My Personal Information." As a developer, you build for the strictest law — Privacy by Design — so your one signup form is legal everywhere at once.

More U.S. State Laws: The American "Patchwork"

There is no single U.S. federal privacy law covering all industries. Instead, each state passes its own rules. California has the most comprehensive state privacy laws in the country:

  • CCPA — California Consumer Privacy Act (2020): Gives California residents the right to know what data is collected about them, the right to delete it, and the right to opt out of having their data sold. Broader than Nevada SB220 — it covers data sharing, not just selling.
  • CPRA — California Privacy Rights Act (2023): An upgrade to CCPA. It created a dedicated enforcement agency (the California Privacy Protection Agency), added new "sensitive personal information" categories, and now requires an opt-in for minors under 16.

Other states — Virginia (CDPA), Colorado (CPA), Connecticut (CTDPA), and Texas (TDPSA) — have all passed similar laws. Because there are so many overlapping state rules, developers target the most restrictive standard to stay compliant everywhere at once.

U.S. Federal Laws: Special-Category Data

Even without a general federal privacy law, Congress has passed laws for specific types of data:

  • COPPA — Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (1998): If your site knowingly collects data from children under age 13, you must get verifiable parental consent first. Violation fines: up to $51,744 per child per incident.
  • HIPAA — Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (1996): Protects PHI (Protected Health Information). If you ever build anything for a doctor's office, hospital, or insurance company, HIPAA governs every line of code you write. Violations can result in criminal charges.
  • CAN-SPAM Act (2003): Governs commercial email marketing. Every marketing email must include a real physical mailing address and a working unsubscribe link honored within 10 business days. Sending spam can cost up to $50,120 per email.
Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think "opt-out" means the same protection as "opt-in," just worded differently. It doesn't — opt-out means data collection happens by default and the burden is on the user to find and use a stop-selling link. Opt-in means nothing happens until the user acts first. Mixing these up on the exam, or on a real client site, gets the compliance backwards.

🌎 Key Exam Concepts

  • Opt-In: Data collection blocked by default — user must actively agree (GDPR model)
  • Opt-Out: Data collection allowed by default — user must actively stop it (U.S. state model)
  • Right to Erasure: User can demand their data be permanently deleted
  • PII: Personally Identifiable Information — any data that can identify a specific person
  • Data Minimization: Only collect data you actually need
  • Privacy by Design: Build privacy protection into the system from the start, not as an afterthought

🌎 International Privacy Laws

The Why: Privacy regulation is now a global issue. If a user from any of these countries visits your website and you collect their data, that country's law may apply to you — even if you're sitting in Nevada. This is called extraterritorial jurisdiction, and it's why "I'm just a small local business" is not a legal shield.

The What: Almost every one of these laws uses the opt-in model, but the specific rights, penalties, and cultural reasoning behind them differ. Compare them below, then read the synthesis after the table.

🇪🇺 UK GDPR 2021

Full Name: United Kingdom General Data Protection Regulation

Model: Opt-In

Key Right: Right to Erasure ("Right to be Forgotten")

Penalty: £17.5 million or 4% of annual turnover

After Brexit, the UK adopted its own version of the EU GDPR — nearly identical in rights and penalties. Companies must now satisfy both if serving UK and EU users.
🇨🇦 Canada PIPEDA / Law 25 2000 / 2022

Full Name: Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act

Model: Opt-In (meaningful consent)

Key Right: Right to access and correct personal data

Penalty: Up to $100,000 CAD per violation

Canada's federal privacy law. Quebec's Law 25 (2022) raised the bar further — requiring privacy impact assessments and data minimization, mirroring GDPR-like standards.
🇧🇷 Brazil LGPD 2020

Full Name: Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados (General Data Protection Law)

Model: Opt-In

Key Right: Right to deletion and data portability

Penalty: Up to 2% of Brazilian revenue (R$50M max)

Brazil's LGPD was closely modeled on GDPR. Brazil is the world's 6th most populous country — any global site likely serves Brazilian users and must comply.
🇯🇵 Japan APPI 2003 / 2022

Full Name: Act on the Protection of Personal Information

Model: Opt-In

Key Right: Right to disclosure and correction

Penalty: Up to ¥100 million (~$650K USD)

Major 2022 amendments brought APPI closer to GDPR: added a right to request deletion, breach notification requirements within 30 days, and expanded to cover pseudonymous data.
🇦🇺 Australia Privacy Act 1988 / 2023

Full Name: Privacy Act 1988 + Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)

Model: Opt-In (implied consent)

Key Right: Right to access and correct personal information

Penalty: Up to AUD $50 million per serious breach

The 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) set strict data handling rules. Penalties tripled in 2023 following major data breaches at Optus and Medibank affecting millions of Australians.
🇨🇳 China PIPL 2021

Full Name: Personal Information Protection Law

Model: Opt-In (explicit separate consent)

Key Right: Right to withdraw consent and data portability

Penalty: ¥50 million or 5% annual revenue

One of the world's strictest laws. Requires data localization — data about Chinese citizens must often stay on servers inside China. Extraterritorial reach: applies to any company processing data of Chinese residents.
🇿🇦 South Africa POPIA 2021

Full Name: Protection of Personal Information Act

Model: Opt-In

Key Right: Right to access, correction, and deletion

Penalty: Up to R10 million (~$550K USD) + possible imprisonment

Africa's most comprehensive privacy law. POPIA covers all personal information processing in South Africa and is enforced by the Information Regulator. Criminal penalties make it unusually strict.
🇹🇭 Thailand / 🇸🇬 Singapore PDPA 2012–2022

Full Name: Personal Data Protection Act

Model: Opt-In (consent-based)

Key Right: Right to withdraw consent; right to erasure

Penalty: Thailand: ฿5M / Singapore: SGD $1 million

Both Thailand (enforced 2022) and Singapore (2012, amended 2020) adopted laws named "PDPA" based on similar consent-first frameworks — requiring purpose limitation and breach notification.
🇮🇳 India DPDP Act 2023

Full Name: Digital Personal Data Protection Act

Model: Opt-In (consent-based)

Key Right: Right to correction, erasure, and grievance redressal

Penalty: Up to ₹250 crore (~$30M USD)

India's first comprehensive data protection law, passed in 2023. With 1.4 billion people, India is now a major jurisdiction any global platform must consider. Rules around cross-border data transfers are still being finalized.

The How — Comparing and Contrasting: Line these laws up and a pattern emerges. Almost every country outside the United States chose the opt-in model — data collection is culturally treated as an invasion until the user explicitly allows it. The United States is the global outlier, defaulting to opt-out at both the federal and state level, reflecting a culture that historically prioritizes commercial speed over individual consent. China's PIPL adds a layer no Western law has: data localization, requiring citizen data to physically stay on servers inside the country — a reflection of national security priorities, not just consumer protection. The penalty structures tell a similar cultural story: the EU and UK peg fines to a percentage of global revenue (making them scale with the size of the offender), while most U.S. laws use flat per-violation dollar amounts.

🆕 Privacy by Design: The Developer's Golden Rule

Because laws differ in almost every country, the professional approach is to build for the strictest standard globally from day one. This principle — Privacy by Design — means baking privacy into your architecture before you write a single line of data collection, rather than bolting it on afterward. Key practices:

  • Data Minimization: Only collect data you absolutely need for the feature to function.
  • Consent by Default: No data collection until the user explicitly agrees (Opt-In everywhere, not just where legally required).
  • Right to Erasure: Build a "delete my account" feature that actually deletes — including backups — from the start.
  • Breach Notification: Have a plan to notify users within 72 hours of discovering a data breach (GDPR requirement; best practice everywhere).
  • Data Localization Awareness: Some laws (China PIPL, Russia) require certain data to stay on servers within that country — a critical consideration for global deployments.
Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think a small local business like Marisol's tamale truck is exempt from international laws because "she's not a global company." Extraterritorial jurisdiction doesn't care about company size — it cares about who visits the site. One tourist from the EU is enough to trigger GDPR's rules for that visitor's data.

Make the Web for Everyone: Accessibility (a11y)

The Why: Remember Devon, Marisol's regular customer who is blind and uses a screen reader? If her ordering page isn't built to work with assistive technology, Devon can't order lunch — and Marisol just lost a paying customer for a reason that had nothing to do with her tamales. Worse, it can be illegal.

The What: In the United States, the ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act) makes accessibility a legal requirement for "places of public accommodation," which courts now say include websites. Nevada businesses must follow it, but the ADA is only the U.S. piece — other regions have their own binding rules: the EU's European Accessibility Act and standard EN 301 549, Canada's AODA (Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act), and the UK's Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations. All of them point developers to the same technical standard: the WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines), divided into Level A (Minimum), Level AA (Standard/Legal Target), and Level AAA (Maximum).

The POUR Principles: The 4 Pillars of a11y

The How: To be accessible, a website must meet these four criteria established by the W3C WAI:

  • Perceivable: Users must be able to perceive the information. If it's visual, there must be a text alternative (Alt Text). If it's audio, there must be captions.
  • Operable: The interface cannot require interactions a user cannot perform. A site that requires a mouse is not operable for Devon or a user with tremors. Everything must be reachable via keyboard.
  • Understandable: Both the information and the operation of the interface must be understandable. Don't use confusing navigation or jargon that hides "what happens next."
  • Robust: Content must work with a wide variety of assistive technologies, including screen readers (NVDA, JAWS, or VoiceOver — the exact software Devon uses).
Who Are You Actually Designing For?

"Accessibility" can sound like one big vague category. It isn't. Web accessibility guidelines are written to cover four broad categories of disability, and a fix for one doesn't automatically fix another:

  • Visual: Blindness, low vision, or color blindness. Barriers: missing alt text, poor contrast, color-only indicators (like a red vs. green error with no icon or text).
  • Auditory: Deafness or hearing loss. Barriers: video with no captions, audio-only alerts with no visual equivalent.
  • Motor/Physical: Limited fine motor control, tremors, or paralysis, which may make a mouse unusable. Barriers: tiny click targets, interactions that require precise mouse movement, no keyboard support.
  • Cognitive/Learning: Conditions like dyslexia, ADHD, or memory-related disabilities. Barriers: dense unbroken text, confusing navigation, auto-playing content, and time limits that are too short.

On Marisol's site, this gets concrete fast: Devon (visual) needs alt text and a screen-reader-friendly menu structure. A customer with a hearing impairment needs captions if a promo video ever gets added. A customer with tremors needs large, well-spaced buttons that don't require pixel-perfect clicking. A customer with ADHD needs a menu that's scannable, not a wall of text — which is exactly the Curb Cut Effect you'll read about later in this chapter.

The Guild Audit: Checking for the "Big 3"
  • Alt Text for Images: Screen readers read code aloud. If Marisol's logo's alt text is empty or says "logo.png," Devon has no idea which site she's on. Rule: Use descriptive text (e.g., alt="Vega's Tamale Co. Logo") and use alt="" for purely decorative images so screen readers skip them.
  • Color Contrast: Use a 4.5:1 ratio for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Use the WebAIM Contrast Checker to verify your palette.
  • Keyboard Navigation: Users who can't use a mouse must be able to "tab" through your site. If Devon can't reach the "Order Now" button with a keyboard, the site is broken. Always ensure there is a visible Focus Ring.
The Technical Edge: ARIA and Semantic HTML

The easiest way to be accessible is Semantic HTML (using <button> instead of <div onclick="...">). When standard HTML isn't enough, we use ARIA (Accessible Rich Internet Applications) roles and attributes to tell assistive technology exactly what a custom component does.

Case Study — Research the ADA in Practice: The Domino's Lawsuit. Domino's Pizza was sued because a blind user couldn't order a pizza on their site using a screen reader — the same problem Devon would face on an inaccessible version of Marisol's site. The case went all the way to the Supreme Court, and Domino's lost. This is the single clearest proof, in current U.S. case law, that accessibility is not a choice; it is a civil right, enforceable under the ADA.

Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often treat "accessibility" as a single checkbox — "I added alt text, so I'm accessible" — and think it only matters for government or "big company" sites. Alt text solves a visual barrier only; it does nothing for a deaf user who needs captions or a user with tremors who needs bigger buttons. And the Domino's case proves size doesn't matter: a private, commercial pizza chain lost at the Supreme Court, so a food truck's website can be held to the same standard.

Your Digital Compass: Ethics and NDAs

The Why: Laws define the floor — the minimum you must do. Ethics define the ceiling — what you should do. A site can be 100% legal and still be designed to manipulate people, and that gap is exactly where dark patterns live.

The What: To model legal and ethical use of information means citing your sources, respecting privacy, and refusing to build Dark Patterns — UI designs intended to trick users into choices they didn't mean to make, like "Sneak into Basket" fees or "Forced Continuity" subscriptions.

Dark Patterns: The Villain's Toolkit

The How: Dark patterns aren't accidents — they are intentional design traps. Designers deliberately use confusing layouts, misleading copy, or hidden options to manipulate users into purchases, subscriptions, or data-sharing they never intended. These tactics may boost short-term conversion, but they destroy long-term trust and can violate consumer protection laws. Hover each card to learn more.

Roach Motel

The Trap: Easy to check in, nearly impossible to check out. Companies make signing up a one-click process but require a letter, phone call, or 7-step buried workflow to cancel.

Example: A streaming service subscribes you in 30 seconds but requires navigating to Account › Billing › Subscriptions › Manage › Cancel to leave.

Misdirection

The Trap: Bright colors or large visuals pull the user's eye to an attractive element while disguising the real action being taken — like a pre-checked "I agree to charges" box tucked out of view.

Example: A giant "CONTINUE FOR FREE" button overshadows a tiny pre-checked box that auto-enrolls you in a paid plan after 30 days.

Trick Questions

The Trap: Double-negative language in checkboxes deliberately confuses users reading quickly. They check or uncheck the opposite of their intent.

Example: "Uncheck this box if you do NOT wish to NOT receive marketing emails." — What does checking it actually mean?

Hidden Costs (Sneak into Basket)

The Trap: Fees, "service charges," or optional add-ons are silently added to the cart. The real total is only revealed at the final payment screen — after the user has already invested time browsing.

Example: A $15 concert ticket becomes $38 with "facility fees," "processing charges," and insurance visible only at checkout.

Forced Continuity (The "Free Trial" Trap)

The Trap: A free trial requires a credit card and automatically converts to a paid subscription on Day 31 — with no reminder email, and a cancellation process buried three menus deep in account settings.

Example: A "Try 30 Days FREE" promotion charges your card on Day 31 with zero warning and makes cancellation intentionally difficult to find.

The Agency Liability Rule: The FTC (Federal Trade Commission) actively pursues dark pattern cases. In 2023, the FTC sued Amazon for $25 million over its Prime cancellation process — a textbook Roach Motel. Building dark patterns for a client does not just expose them to lawsuits; it exposes your agency as the designer to liability as well.

Try it yourself: If Marisol asks you to make her "join our email list" checkbox pre-checked so more people sign up, that's a Misdirection dark pattern. The ethical, and legal, response is to leave it unchecked and explain to her why — that's what it means to model ethical use of information in a real client relationship, not just in a textbook example.

The Secret-Keeper's Oath: Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDA)

The Why: When Marisol hires your agency, she'll hand over her supplier list, her recipes, and her financial numbers. If that leaked to a competitor, it could genuinely end her business. She needs a legal guarantee you won't talk — that guarantee is an NDA.

The What: A Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA) is more than a "pinky promise" 🤫. It is a legally binding contract that creates a "confidential relationship" between you and an employer or client. It is the primary tool used to protect Trade Secrets — proprietary information that gives a business a competitive edge.

Anatomy of a Professional NDA

The How: When you sign your first contract for an internship or job, expect these standard clauses:

  • The Scope of Confidentiality: Defines exactly what is secret. Often includes source code, internal logic, UI mockups, client lists, and unreleased project codenames.
  • Unilateral vs. Mutual: A Unilateral NDA is one-way (you keep their secrets). A Mutual NDA is two-way (you both keep each other's secrets, common in startup partnerships).
  • The Term (Survival Clause): Secrets don't always expire. Most NDAs last 2 to 5 years, but Trade Secrets (like a secret tamale recipe, or the Google Search algorithm) are protected indefinitely. Even after you leave a job, your obligation "survives" the end of employment.
  • Remedies for Breach: If you leak information, the company can seek Injunctive Relief (a court order to stop you immediately) and Liquidated Damages (pre-set massive fines).
The "Blackball" and Real-World Scenarios

If you break an NDA — for example, by leaking a screenshot of a new game level to Discord — you aren't just fired. You can be sued for massive sums, and you will likely be blackballed from the industry. Tech is a small world; your reputation for keeping secrets is your most valuable asset.

  • Scenario A: The "GTA VI" Leak. Leaking footage or code of an unreleased high-profile game can result in criminal charges and civil lawsuits for millions in lost marketing value.
  • Scenario B: The Freelancer's Logic. You cannot take a secret encryption algorithm from a fintech client and sell it to their competitor. This is "Theft of Trade Secrets."
  • Scenario C: The Interview Leak. Many companies (like Google or Apple) require you to sign an NDA just to interview so you don't leak their internal test questions or future plans.
  • Scenario D: Marisol's Recipe. While building her site, you see her secret marinade recipe written on a sticky note. Posting it publicly, even as a joke, is a textbook trade-secret breach if you've signed her agency NDA.
Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think an NDA's obligations end the moment you stop working for someone. Most NDAs explicitly "survive" termination — your duty to keep the secret can outlast the job by years, and Trade Secrets can be protected indefinitely.

Check Your Bias at the Door

The Why: Code feels neutral — it's just logic, right? It isn't, because the humans who write it and the data that trains it are never neutral. When that shows up in software, real people get hurt in ways that are hard to see from the inside.

The What: We all have biases — shortcuts our brains make based on limited experience. When these human shortcuts enter code, it becomes Algorithmic Bias. Computers are reflective: they learn from the data we give them. Bias usually enters through the Training Dataset. If you build a hiring AI and train it on resumes from the last 10 years (mostly men), the AI "learns" that men are better and docks points for resumes containing the word "women's."

Real-World Case Studies:

The How: These aren't hypothetical — they happened:

  • The Racist Soap Dispenser: Infrared sensors that only activated for light skin tones because they weren't tested on darker skin. Darkness absorbs infrared light; the sensors were "too cheap" to detect it.
  • The Biased Hiring AI: An algorithm that learned "men are better" because it was trained on 10 years of male-dominated resumes.
  • Healthcare Inequity: Algorithms that prioritized white patients over Black patients because they used "previous healthcare cost" as a shortcut for "medical need," ignoring that Black patients had historically been denied access to expensive care.
Inclusive Design: The "Curb Cut" Effect

Inclusive design means building for the most marginalized users to make the experience better for everyone. This is the Curb Cut Effect. Sidewalk ramps (curb cuts) were designed for wheelchairs, but they also benefit parents with strollers and travelers with suitcases. In web design, high contrast helps the vision-impaired, but it also helps the user reading their phone in direct sunlight. Explore the work of the Algorithmic Justice League (AJL) to learn more.

Analyzing Content for Bias — Inclusive Checklist: When you review Marisol's site content, ask:

  • Are gender selections on any form inclusive, or do they force a binary choice that excludes real customers?
  • Do the stock photos represent a diverse range of skin tones and abilities, or only one kind of customer?
  • Is the text written at a readable level for all users, not just fluent English readers?
  • Would this design or copy make any group of real customers feel like an afterthought?
Where Everyone Gets Ambushed: Students often think algorithmic bias requires a "bad" or intentionally racist programmer. It almost never does — it usually comes from a well-meaning team that didn't test on a diverse enough dataset or user group. Bias is a testing failure, not always a malice failure.
